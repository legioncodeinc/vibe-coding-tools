# Getting started with third-party due diligence

- **Publisher:** Diligent (GRC platform vendor)
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** vendor-blog

## The eight-step framework

1. Map the compliance landscape and risk exposure.
2. Define objectives, aligned to financial and strategic goals.
3. Gather documentation: incorporation papers, shareholder information, identification, conflict disclosures.
4. Vet third parties against watch lists, sanction lists, and media coverage.
5. Analyze risk against business context.
6. Document the process.
7. Monitor for emerging red flags.
8. Review and improve the process.

## Risk-based tiering

Tiers exist because "some vendors may introduce more risk than others", which lets diligence intensity vary by relationship type rather than running maximum depth on everything.

## What a diligence file contains

- Self-reported information from questionnaires.
- Watch list and sanction results.
- Media research and press findings.
- **Verification records comparing independent investigation against the counterparty's own claims.**
- Site visit reports and local intelligence at the enhanced tier.

## Red flags

Watch list or sanctions appearances, negative media, corruption or reputational concerns, ethical conflicts, and **"discrepancies between self-reported and independently verified information"**.

## Documentation and audit trail

"Keep extensive records of any information or documentation you gather. This will help prove your regulatory compliance and validate your decisions about all third-party relationships."

## Relevance to a person dossier

Two structural imports. First, **risk-based tiering** is the justification for purpose binding: depth is set by what the relationship warrants, and running maximum depth on a routine prospect is a defect in the process, not thoroughness. Second, the diligence file's "verification records comparing independent investigation against claims" line item, plus the red flag phrased as a discrepancy between self-reported and independently verified information, is precisely the dossier's Reconciliation section. This source establishes that surfacing the gap between what someone says and what the record shows is a standard, expected diligence artifact rather than an adversarial flourish.
