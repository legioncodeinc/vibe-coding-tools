## The two questions

- Primary: **"How do you know that?"** Asked by reporters of sources, by editors of reporters, and asked internally about sources that cannot be questioned directly.
- Secondary: **"How else do you know that?"** This encodes verification as multilayered and demands persistence.

## The three factors in successful verification

1. Individual resourcefulness, persistence, skepticism, and skill.
2. Sources' reliability, honesty, and **variety**.
3. Documentation.

## Do not trust single sources, even honest ones

The handbook's worked example: twelve members of a sports team gave consistent accounts of a past event. The accounts were consistent AND inaccurate, shaped by emotion and shared legend. The author: "I didn't question these consistent memories in my reporting, but learned almost by accident that they were exaggerated." Documentary evidence (video) showed the event differed significantly from the collective memory.

**This is the explicit limit of the two-source rule.** Two sources drawing on a shared narrative are one source. Independence, not headcount, is what corroboration requires.

## Question authoritative sources hardest

Official sources fail too. Cited example: a state governor incorrectly announced twelve miners rescued when only one had survived.

## The core principle

"Our job is not to parrot sources and the material they provide, but to challenge them, triangulate what they provide with other credible sources and verify what is true."

## Relevance to a person dossier

The consistent-and-wrong failure mode is the exact risk in a Littlebird-sourced dossier. A person's LinkedIn headline, their conference bio, their email signature, and their self-introduction in a recorded meeting will all agree, because they all originate from the same person. Four agreeing artifacts is one source. The skill needs a named independence test, not a source count, and the counterparty's own self-description must be graded as a single origin no matter how many surfaces it appears on.
