# Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?

- **Title:** Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?
- **Authors:** Vinu Sankar Sadasivan, Aounon Kumar, Sriram Balasubramanian, Wenxiao Wang,
  Soheil Feizi
- **Venue:** Transactions on Machine Learning Research (TMLR)
- **Dates:** arXiv:2303.11156 submitted 17 March 2023, last revised 17 January 2025
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11156
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic (peer-reviewed venue, TMLR)

## Why this source matters here

Every other detection source in this archive is empirical: it tests tools that exist.
This one argues about the ceiling. It is the reason a skill must never promise
undetectability, and equally the reason it must never promise detectability.

## Findings

### The theoretical result

The paper gives a framework connecting the **AUROC of the best possible detector to the
total variation distance between the human text distribution and the AI text
distribution.** As those two distributions converge, the achievable AUROC of any
detector falls toward chance.

The practical reading: detection difficulty is not an engineering problem that better
detectors solve. It is bounded by how similar machine text has become to human text, and
that similarity has been increasing by design.

### The recursive paraphrasing attack

Running generated text through a paraphraser, recursively, significantly reduces
detection rates across detector families, including neural detectors, zero-shot methods
such as DetectGPT, retrieval-based detectors, and watermarking schemes. The paper reports
that this only slightly degrades text quality in many cases.

Per-detector AUROC drop figures were not extracted in this fetch. The archive records
the qualitative result and marks the numbers as not retrieved.

### The spoofing attack

An attacker can infer hidden AI text signatures **without white-box access to the
detection method**, and use them to make human-written text be classified as AI. The
paper frames this as a reputational risk for LLM developers.

The consequence for an individual writer is worse: it means a false AI accusation can be
manufactured against genuine human writing, and that watermarking does not protect the
accused.

## Claims this source supports

1. There is a theoretical bound on detector performance tied to the total variation
   distance between human and machine text distributions. Better detectors cannot escape
   it, only a persistent distributional gap can sustain them.
2. Recursive paraphrasing significantly reduces detection rates across neural, zero-shot,
   retrieval-based, and watermarking detectors, with only slight quality loss.
3. Watermarking is not a solution: it is defeated by paraphrase and it is spoofable.
4. Human-written text can be made to look AI-generated by an adversary without access to
   the detector.

## Limits of this source for our purpose

- Adversarial framing. This skill is not an evasion tool and does not use the attack
  results as a method.
- Per-detector numeric drops were not retrieved from the abstract page fetched. Anyone
  needing those must read the full paper.
- The theoretical bound is about distributions, not about any particular document.
