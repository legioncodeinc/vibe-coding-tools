# Nudging Attention to Workplace Meeting Goals: A Large-Scale, Preregistered Field Experiment

- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16939v1
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic (arXiv preprint, preregistered field experiment)

## Study design

Preregistered field experiment at a global technology company.

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Participants | 361 (treatment 163, control 198) |
| Meetings tracked | 7,196 (treatment 3,318, control 3,878) |
| Duration | Two weeks |
| Assignment | Randomized to treatment or control |
| Treatment survey pairs completed | Average 11.2 pre and post meeting pairs |
| Control post meeting surveys completed | Average 13.4 |

## The intervention

The treatment group received a brief pre-meeting survey delivered via Microsoft Teams
chat five minutes before each scheduled meeting. Participants picked from a closed list
of 10 meeting goals, for example "Decision is made" and "New ideas are generated", with
an option for a custom entry. The survey framing, quoted:

> "In your understanding, what does success look like for this upcoming meeting?"

Both groups rated meeting effectiveness afterward on a six point scale.

## Findings

**Primary outcome.** The pre-meeting intervention showed no statistically significant
impact on perceived meeting effectiveness between groups.

**Secondary outcomes.** Both treatment and control groups improved on self-reported goal
clarity and communication from onboarding to debriefing, suggesting the post-meeting
survey itself acted as an unintended intervention in both conditions.

Authors' conclusion, quoted:

> "While the intervention impact on meeting effectiveness was not statistically
> significant, mixed-methods findings revealed improvements in self-reported awareness
> and behaviour across both groups."

The paper notes "diversity in people's meeting loads" typical of real world field
experiments, with meetings distributed unevenly across participants.

## Why it matters for this skill

This is the counterweight source. It is direct evidence that a thin, generic, five
minute pre-meeting prompt does NOT move meeting effectiveness. A pre-call brief that is
merely a nudge or a restated agenda has no measured effect. If a brief is going to earn
its place it has to carry substantive content the attendee did not already have, not a
prompt to think about goals.
