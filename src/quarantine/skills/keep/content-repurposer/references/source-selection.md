# Source selection

Not every long-form artifact is worth a week of derivatives. Expanding a bad source produces
a bad pack no matter how good the angles are, and the pack ships under the user's name.

**The gate: a source has to support at least four distinct angles from
`angle-taxonomy.md`. Fewer than four and the pack becomes restatement by arithmetic,
because there is not enough distinct material to go around.**

Practice already contains this step, unnamed. Standard workflow guidance says to identify
"high-value, evergreen, or proven-performing pieces" before doing anything else
(`research/distilled-repurposing-and-format-adaptation.md`, section 4). That is source
selection. It just does not usually get a test attached.

---

## What makes a good source

### 1. One strong idea, not five weak ones

A source arguing one thing well can be looked at from seven directions. A source listing
five unrelated points has five sources inside it and none of them is long enough to expand.

The check: state the source's argument in one sentence. If the sentence needs an "and", the
source is probably two sources. Split it and evaluate each half separately, or pick the
strongest third and treat the rest as out of scope for this pack.

### 2. A specific story with stakes

Not "we work with clients on this". One situation, one time, one outcome, and a point where
something was actually at risk. A specific story feeds the story angle directly and usually
carries a number and a counter-example with it.

The check: can you name when it happened, roughly who it happened to by role, and what went
wrong at some point? Three yeses and the story angle is live.

### 3. Real numbers the user can stand behind

A duration, a count, a cost, a rate, a percentage with a denominator underneath it. The
number angle depends on this entirely and cannot be manufactured.

The check: is the number in the source, in the user's own words, with its hedge intact? A
number the user cannot restate on demand is not a number the pack can publish.

### 4. A position, not a report

A source that argues gives you the claim, the objection, and the contrarian read for free.
A source that reports gives you none of the three, because there is nothing to disagree with.

The check: could a competent person in the user's field read this and disagree? If not,
three of the seven angles are unavailable before you start.

### 5. Durable rather than dated

A pack ships over one to two weeks. A source whose relevance expires inside that window
produces derivatives that expire mid-sequence.

## What makes a bad source, and what to do instead

| Bad source | Why it fails | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| **A list of five unrelated points** | Each point gets one thin derivative. The pack has no through line and the angles collapse into "here is point three". Named in practice as idea dilution, where fragmenting weakens each piece (`research/distilled-repurposing-and-format-adaptation.md`, section 1). | Pick the single strongest point and expand THAT. The other four are future sources. |
| **A time-sensitive announcement** | Announcements are news, and news has one angle: it happened. Sequencing a pack over two weeks means the last pieces publish after it stopped being true. | Publish it once, natively, now. Do not repurpose it. |
| **A pure roundup or link post** | The content belongs to other people. Every derivative is a restatement of someone else's claim and the user's name goes on it. | Skip. If the user has a position ABOUT the roundup, that position is the source. |
| **A source with no specific detail** | Generic advice produces generic derivatives. There is no story, no number, and no counter-example, so four of seven angles are dead. | Ask the user for the incident behind the advice. That incident is the real source. |
| **Something already repurposed** | The pack becomes derivatives of derivatives, drifting further from the original detail each pass. | Check the run log. Go back to the original artifact, or skip. |
| **A source whose good parts are confidential** | The screen removes the specific detail and leaves the generic frame, which is now a bad source. | Screen BEFORE selecting, not after drafting. A source that only works with the confidential material in it is not a source. |
| **A promotional piece** | A sales post has one claim by design. Seven angles on a pitch is seven pitches. | Skip. |

## The four-angle audit, which is the actual gate

Before committing to a source, run the taxonomy against it and write down what you find.

For each of the seven angles in `angle-taxonomy.md`, answer one question: **does this source
contain the material this angle needs?**

| Angle | Material it needs | Present? |
|---|---|---|
| The claim | A single defensible position | |
| The counter-example | An exception, edge case, or stated boundary | |
| The how | A repeatable sequence with real steps | |
| The story behind it | One incident with a time, a role, and stakes | |
| The objection | An objection the user has actually heard | |
| The specific number | A figure with its hedge intact | |
| The contrarian read | A position most of the field does not hold | |

**Four or more present: proceed.**

**Exactly three: report it to the user as a thin source before proceeding.** Offer three
options: a smaller pack of three or four pieces, combining this source with a second one, or
picking a different source. Their call, taken before the drafting work happens.

**Two or fewer: do not proceed.** Say which angles are missing and why. Suggest what would
make the source viable, usually one incident or one number the user has and did not include.

Do not pad. Inventing a counter-example to reach four is worse than reporting three,
because the invented one ships under the user's name and a reader who knows the domain will
spot it.

## Source type notes

### A call transcript

The rebuild from spoken to written register is a different craft problem with real research
behind it, and it is already solved in the sibling skill. **Do not restate it here.** Read
`../../said-it-already/references/spoken-to-written.md` and apply it before angling
anything.

The short version of why it matters: spoken and written registers barely share building
blocks, ordinary speech runs about 6% disfluent, and the speaker doing the explaining is
measurably MORE disfluent than the listener, so the highest-value passage in a transcript is
usually the roughest one on the page. That guide has the measurements and the cut-keep-rebuild
tables.

Attribution applies before anything else. A transcript proves a line was said, not who said
it. Raw chunks are frequently tagged `[Others]`
(`littlebird-mcp-reference.md`, known limitations). Take wording from a transcript and
attribution from the structured summary's `## For You`, `## Decisions`, and `## Action
Items` sections. The sibling skill's
`../../said-it-already/references/attribution-screening.md` has the surface ladder.

### A webinar or a Loom

Same spoken-to-written problem, plus one addition: the visual carried part of the argument.
A step the user pointed at on screen is not in the transcript. Flag any passage where the
words alone do not carry the point, and either ask the user what was on screen or drop that
passage.

### A long email

Usually the best source type in this list. It is already written register, the user
definitely wrote it, and it was addressed to one person, which tends to make it specific.

Two cautions. It was written TO someone, so it carries shared context a public reader does
not have. And it is private correspondence, so the recipient and anything about their
situation goes through the confidentiality screen before anything else.

### A Facebook post or an existing public post

Already public, already the user's words, already in written register. The lowest-risk
source type.

The trap: because it is already good and already public, the temptation is to reformat
rather than re-angle. That is cosmetic variation
(`research/distilled-repurposing-and-format-adaptation.md`, section 1), and it is the exact
failure the angle taxonomy exists to prevent. A source that already works still gets seven
different claims, not one claim at five lengths.

## Performance signal on the source, and why it is weak

If the user's Littlebird capture crossed the original post's engagement, it is worth
looking at. It is worth very little.

**What can be observed:** reaction counts, comment counts, share counts, and named
commenters, all as they appeared on screen at capture time
(`littlebird-mcp-reference.md`, verified capture receipts).

**Why it is nearly useless as a selection criterion.**

1. **It is partial by construction.** Social UIs collapse lists into "and 4 others" and "12
   people reacted". Any roster built from that capture is incomplete and must be reported
   as incomplete, with the named set, the count of unnamed entries, and where the gap came
   from (`evidence-standards.md`, rule 5).
2. **It is a snapshot, not a total.** A capture taken two hours after posting and a capture
   taken a week later are different numbers for the same post, and there is usually no way
   to tell which one you have.
3. **It is not comparable.** Two posts captured at different ages under different follower
   counts cannot be ranked against each other.

**How to use it anyway.** As a weak tiebreaker between two sources that both pass the
four-angle audit, and never as the reason to choose a source that failed it. State it as
observed with a receipt, state the collapsed count, and say plainly that engagement from
capture is partial and collapsed. Never present it as the post's performance.

Never write "your best-performing post". Write "the post whose captured reaction count was
highest among the three found, noting that captured counts are snapshots and collapse
unnamed reactors".
