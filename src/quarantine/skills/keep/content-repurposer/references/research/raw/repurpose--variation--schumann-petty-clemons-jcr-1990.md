# Predicting the Effectiveness of Different Strategies of Advertising Variation

- **Title:** Predicting the Effectiveness of Different Strategies of Advertising Variation: A Test of the Repetition-Variation Hypotheses
- **Authors:** David W. Schumann, Richard E. Petty, D. Scott Clemons
- **Journal:** Journal of Consumer Research, vol. 17, no. 2, September 1990, pp. 192-202
- **URL:** https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/Schuman_1990.pdf
- **Published:** 1990
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic (peer-reviewed, Journal of Consumer Research)

## Why this source is outside the 6-month window and kept anyway

The default research window for this archive is the last 6 months. This paper is from
1990. It is kept because it is the only peer-reviewed experimental evidence found in the
whole sweep that directly tests the question this skill is built around: when you put the
same message in front of the same person repeatedly, does changing the LOOK help, or do
you have to change the ARGUMENT. Nothing published in the last 6 months tests that
question. Every recent source in this archive asserts an answer without measuring one.

## Extracted content

### The distinction that matters

**Cosmetic variation:** "certain nonsubstantive features of the ads are altered while the
basic product message is kept the same." Named features include color, graphics, fonts,
and layout.

**Substantive variation:** "a change in message content (i.e., arguments, attributes)"
while the cosmetic characteristics stay constant.

### Method

Two experiments, both 2x2x2 factorial designs, grounded in the Elaboration Likelihood
Model.

- **Study 1, cosmetic variation.** 294 undergraduates plus 64 controls. Factors: product
  relevance (high or low), variation (same ad repeated or cosmetically varied ads),
  repetition (4 or 8 exposures).
- **Study 2, substantive variation.** 200 undergraduates plus 40 controls. Factors:
  relevance (high or low), variation (same or substantively varied), repetition (3 or 5
  exposures).

### Findings

**Study 1 (cosmetic).** Under LOW product relevance and moderate repetition, "Subjects who
were exposed to the varied ad campaign liked the product and advertising significantly
more."

**Study 2 (substantive).** Under HIGH relevance, where subjects were motivated to process
the content, "Subjects who viewed the substantively varied ads and were motivated to
process them" "rated the product and advertising campaign significantly higher."

## Claims this source supports

1. Varying the surface presentation of an identical message helps only with a low-attention
   audience that is not processing the argument. This is the measured case FOR reformatting
   alone, and it is narrow.
2. With a motivated, attentive audience, the variation that works is a change in the
   ARGUMENT, not a change in the packaging. This is the single strongest piece of evidence
   in the archive for the angle taxonomy being the core of the skill.
3. A person who follows one operator on several surfaces is by definition the
   high-relevance, motivated-processing case. Cosmetic variation is the wrong tool for that
   audience.
4. The framing "same idea in five shapes" is cosmetic variation applied to an audience for
   which the experiment found cosmetic variation does not carry the effect.

## Caveats to carry forward

- Undergraduate lab subjects, print and broadcast advertising, 1990. It is not social
  media and it is not personal content. The mechanism (motivated processing rewards
  argument change) transfers plausibly; the effect sizes do not transfer at all.
- The experiments measured product and campaign attitude, not reach, follows, or leads.
- Repetition counts are 3 to 8 exposures inside a study session, not a week of posting.

State the transfer as an inference, never as a measured finding about social posting.
