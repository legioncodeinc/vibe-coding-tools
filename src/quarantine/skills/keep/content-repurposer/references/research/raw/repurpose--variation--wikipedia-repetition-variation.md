## Why this source is here

It is a tertiary source and carries no weight on its own. It is archived for exactly one
reason: it is the entry point that located the Schumann/Petty/Clemons primary paper, and it
confirms that the cosmetic-versus-substantive distinction is a named, established construct
in the advertising literature rather than a framing invented for this skill. Every
substantive claim drawn from this area cites the primary paper, not this file.

## Extracted content

Repetition variation is described as an approach that "modifies repeated ads to maintain
consumer interest and effectiveness while avoiding overexposure", with the stated purpose
of combating wearout, meaning reduced engagement from overly repetitive messaging.

Two named methods:

- **Cosmetic variation:** minor adjustments such as color or visual tweaks that preserve
  the core message.
- **Substantive variation:** significant changes in themes, narratives, or formats that
  create fresh experiences while maintaining brand identity.

Studies named in the entry:

- **Schumann and Clemons (1989)**, "Repetition/Variation Hypotheses". Note: the primary
  paper located and archived separately is Schumann, Petty and Clemons, Journal of Consumer
  Research, 1990. The entry's citation year and author list are both slightly off, which is
  itself a reason to cite the primary rather than this entry.
- **Guido (2012)**, "The Salience of Marketing Stimuli", reported as finding that varied
  marketing stimuli enhance brand recall and reduce ad fatigue. **Not independently
  verified in this sweep. Do not cite this finding in the skill.**

## Claims this source supports

1. Cosmetic versus substantive variation is established terminology, not a coinage.
2. "Wearout" is the established name for the failure mode this skill is designed to avoid.
