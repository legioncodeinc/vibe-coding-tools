# Sequencing the pack

A content pack is not a pile of drafts. It is an order, and the order is part of the
deliverable. **Every sequencing plan states which piece goes out when AND why that order,
because an order with no reasoning is a schedule, not a plan.**

---

## What sequencing is for, and what it is not for

### It is not the fix for repetition

Standard practice prescribes spacing repurposed content across time and varying formats to
prevent repetition (`research/distilled-repurposing-and-format-adaptation.md`, section 4).

**That is a scheduling remedy for an argument problem.** Spacing five restatements over two
weeks makes the repetition slower, not smaller. The reader who follows the user on three
surfaces still gets the same claim three times, just further apart, and they still notice.

The angle taxonomy is the fix for repetition. Sequencing is for something else.

### What it is actually for

Three things, in priority order.

1. **Establishing the claim before the pieces that depend on it.** The counter-example and
   the objection both assume the reader has met the claim. Publishing the objection first
   asks a reader to defend a position nobody has stated.
2. **Spacing the surfaces a single reader sees.** The person following on LinkedIn and
   Facebook and email is the reader most at risk from a pack. Spread their exposure.
3. **Reserving room for what the pack learns.** A piece that gets a real objection in the
   comments changes what the later pieces should say. A fully pre-scheduled pack cannot use
   that.

## The cadence evidence, and its limits

One credible measurement exists and it covers one platform.

Buffer analyzed more than 2 million LinkedIn posts from more than 94,000 accounts, using
z-score analysis of each account's high-frequency weeks against its own baseline plus fixed
effects regression to control for account-level differences
(`research/distilled-repurposing-and-format-adaptation.md`, section 4). The within-account
design is what makes it worth citing: it compares an account to itself rather than comparing
large accounts to small ones.

Findings as stated (same source):

| Posts per week | Reported effect |
|---|---|
| 2 to 5 | plus 1,182 impressions per post, plus 0.23 percentage points engagement |
| 6 to 10 | plus 5,001 impressions per post, plus 0.76 percentage points |
| 11 or more | "nearly 17,000 more impressions per post, 3x more engagements" |

Headline recommendation: 2 to 5 times weekly is "the sweet spot for improving reach *and*
engagement" (same). On penalties: "LinkedIn doesn't 'cap' your reach or punish you for
posting often" (same).

### Three things to take from this and one not to

**Take:**

1. A weekly pack of 5 to 7 short pieces on one surface is inside the sweet spot and exceeds
   no cap this analysis found.
2. **The argument for restraint is a reader argument, not an algorithm argument.** This is
   the correction that matters most. The folk wisdom that posting often gets you throttled
   is not supported by the one measurement in the archive. Do not tell a user to slow down
   for algorithmic reasons.
3. Cadence in this domain is measurable, and one credible measurement exists.

**Do not take:** that repetition is harmless. This analysis did not assess whether the
high-frequency accounts were repeating themselves (same source, section 4). It cannot
distinguish a high-variety account from a spammy one and therefore says nothing in either
direction about the problem this skill exists to solve.

**Named gap: no comparable cadence measurement was found for Facebook, X, Instagram, or
email** (same source, section 7). Do not transfer the LinkedIn numbers. Where a user asks
about cadence on another surface, say the evidence is not there and ask what they have been
doing.

## The default sequence

A working default for a pack of 5 to 7 short posts, one carousel, one email, and one thread,
spread over 7 to 10 days. Adjust to the user's surfaces and their existing rhythm. State the
reasoning for every slot.

| Slot | Day | Piece | Angle | Why here |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Day 1 | Short post, primary surface | The claim | Everything downstream references this position. It goes first so the later pieces are not arguing with a stranger. |
| 2 | Day 2 | Short post, second surface | The specific number | Concrete and short. Lands the claim in evidence while it is fresh, on a different surface so the overlapping reader is not hit twice on one feed. |
| 3 | Day 3 | Thread | The how | The first substantial piece. A procedure earns the length a thread asks for. |
| 4 | Day 4 or 5 | Short post | The counter-example | Placed after the claim has had time to draw agreement. This is the piece that makes the user look like they have thought about it rather than pitched it. |
| 5 | Day 5 | Email | The story behind it | Email tolerates a slow start and length better than any feed. It is also the lowest-overlap surface, so it can carry the longest version. |
| 6 | Day 7 | Carousel | The how, expanded, or a second claim | Carousels take production time. Placing it late means the pack's early responses can shape it. |
| 7 | Day 8 | Short post | The objection | By now real objections may have appeared in comments. This slot exists to absorb one. |
| 8 | Day 10 | Short post | The contrarian read | Last, deliberately. The riskiest piece publishes after the pack has established the user was being reasonable. |

### The rules behind that table

1. **The claim goes first.** Non-negotiable. Three of the seven angles assume the reader has
   met the position.
2. **The contrarian read goes last.** Published first, it defines the user as someone who
   picks fights. Published last, it reads as a conclusion.
3. **Consecutive slots never share a surface.** The multi-surface follower is the reader this
   pack most endangers.
4. **The two production-heavy pieces, the carousel and the email, go mid-to-late.** They
   take longer to make and they benefit from what the early pieces learn.
5. **At least one slot is reserved for response.** Slot 7 in the default. A pack with nothing
   held back cannot react to its own audience.
6. **The story piece goes to email or the longest-tolerant surface available.** A story
   compressed to 280 characters is an anecdote with the stakes cut out.

## Adjusting the sequence

### If the user posts on only one surface

The spacing rule loses its purpose and the fatigue risk climbs, because every piece hits the
same reader in the same feed.

Spread to 10 to 14 days, cut to 4 or 5 pieces, and hold the angles further apart. Under the
one relevant experiment, the same-surface audience is the high-relevance case and cosmetic
variation is exactly the wrong tool there
(`research/distilled-repurposing-and-format-adaptation.md`, section 1).

### If the user has an established cadence

Fit the pack into it. Do not propose a schedule that changes their posting rhythm as a side
effect of one pack. If their normal rhythm is two posts a week, an 8-piece pack is a
four-week sequence, not a reason to post daily.

### If the source is losing relevance

Compress. Move the claim, the number, and the how to the front three days and drop the
lowest-value angles rather than publishing a stale pack slowly. A source that needs
compressing this hard probably failed source selection.

### If the pack is going out alongside other content

Sequencing plans an insertion, not a takeover. Ask what else is scheduled and thread the
pack between it. Standard practice suggests a mix rather than an all-repurposed calendar,
with one unsourced convention naming roughly 30% repurposed to 70% new
(`research/distilled-repurposing-and-format-adaptation.md`, section 4). Cite the ratio as one
practitioner's convention if it comes up. Do not present it as a target.

## What ships in the sequencing plan

A table with these columns, and the last one is the point:

| # | Send day | Surface | Piece | Angle | Why this slot |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Plus three lines underneath:

- **Held for response.** Which slot is reserved and what it would absorb.
- **Overlap note.** Which surfaces a single follower is most likely to see together, and how
  the sequence spaces them.
- **Cadence basis.** Whether the sequence fits the user's existing rhythm, and the plain
  statement that the only cadence measurement in the research covers LinkedIn and does not
  transfer to other surfaces.

**Nothing in this plan is scheduled by the skill.** The plan is a recommendation the user
executes. Nothing publishes and nothing is written into a scheduling tool without the user
approving the actual final text of each piece.
