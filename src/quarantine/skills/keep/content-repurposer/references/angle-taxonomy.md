# The angle taxonomy

The craft core of this skill. Everything else is logistics.

**The operating rule: every derivative in a pack takes a DIFFERENT ANGLE on the source. Not
a different format of the same angle.**

A pack that fails this rule is the standard failure of content repurposing. It reads as
spam to anyone who follows the user on more than one surface, which is precisely the
audience the user most wants to keep.

---

## Why this is the design decision the skill lives or dies on

### The evidence, such as it is

One controlled experiment in the research archive tests this directly. It distinguishes
**cosmetic variation**, meaning "certain nonsubstantive features of the ads are altered
while the basic product message is kept the same", from **substantive variation**, meaning
"a change in message content (i.e., arguments, attributes)"
(`research/distilled-repurposing-and-format-adaptation.md`, section 1).

The result that matters: cosmetic variation carried under LOW message relevance. Substantive
variation carried under HIGH relevance, with subjects "motivated to process them" (same).

A person who follows one operator on LinkedIn and Facebook and their email list is the
high-relevance case by construction. They opted in more than once. For that reader, the one
experiment available points at changing the argument, not the packaging.

**Say this out loud when using the taxonomy: that is an inference.** The study is from 1990,
tested print and broadcast advertising on undergraduates, and measured product attitude
across 3 to 8 exposures in a session (same source, section 1). The mechanism transfers
plausibly. Nothing about the effect size transfers.

### What the craft literature says, and why it is not enough

The best trade-press advice available prescribes "Change the packaging, not the message" and
"Each format needs its own hook"
(`research/distilled-repurposing-and-format-adaptation.md`, section 1). Read against the
experiment, that is a recommendation to use cosmetic variation on an engaged audience.

The one source in the archive devoted entirely to repurposing without repeating offers three
angles: a pain point angle, a question angle, and a story angle (same). All three are
OPENING DEVICES. They are three doors into the same room. A reader who walks through all
three arrives at the same claim three times and notices.

**No source in the archive supplies a taxonomy that changes the claim.** The seven angles
below are authored. They extend the cosmetic-versus-substantive distinction, which is
sourced. Say which is which when the user asks where this came from.

---

## The two tests

Run both on every pack before it ships. They are cheap and they catch the failure.

### Test 1: the one-sentence claim test

Write each derivative's claim as a single declarative sentence, without its hook, its
format, or its examples. Strip it to the assertion.

Line up the sentences.

- If two sentences are paraphrases of each other, **those two pieces are the same angle** no
  matter how different they look. Kill one or re-angle it.
- If a sentence cannot be written, the piece has no claim and is decoration. Kill it.

Worked example of a pack that FAILS. Source: a Facebook post about a client project that
went badly because nobody owned it.

| Piece | Claim sentence |
|---|---|
| LinkedIn post | Projects fail because of ownership, not tooling. |
| Carousel | Five reasons projects fail, and ownership is the real one. |
| Email | The tool was never the problem. Ownership was. |
| Thread | Here is how a project fails when nobody owns it. |
| X post | It is never the tool. It is ownership. |

Five paraphrases. Five formats. One angle. This is the pack the skill exists to prevent.

The same source, PASSING:

| Piece | Angle | Claim sentence |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn post | The claim | Projects fail from unowned decisions, not from bad tooling. |
| X post | The counter-example | The best-run project I have seen used the worst tool in the category. |
| Carousel | The how | Here are the six decisions that need a named owner before a project starts. |
| Email | The story behind it | I lost eleven weeks learning this and here is the week it went wrong. |
| Short post | The objection | "We do have an owner" is usually true and usually the wrong owner. |
| Short post | The specific number | Eleven weeks. That is what the missing owner cost. |
| Short post | The contrarian read | Most of the tool-selection advice in this category is solving the wrong problem. |

Seven claims. A reader can agree with one and argue with another. That is variety.

### Test 2: the disagreement test

For each pair of pieces in the pack, ask: **could an intelligent reader agree with one and
disagree with the other?**

If not, they are one piece in two costumes.

This test is stricter than the claim test and catches the near-paraphrase that the claim
test lets through. "It is never the tool" and "ownership is what breaks projects" are
different sentences, but nobody agrees with one and disputes the other.

## The seven angles

Each entry gives what it asserts, what the source must contain for it to be usable, its
failure mode, and its natural formats. The angle is chosen for the CONTENT it needs, then a
format is chosen for the angle. Never the other way round.

---

### 1. The claim

**Asserts:** the central thing the source argues, stated flat, with no build-up and no
hedging beyond what the source hedged.

**Needs from the source:** a single defensible position. If the source has three positions,
this angle picks one and the other two become their own pieces.

**Failure mode:** turning into a summary. A summary says what the source covered. A claim
says what is true. If the piece contains the words "I wrote about" or "in my recent post",
it has become a teaser and is no longer standing alone.

**Natural formats:** the shortest surface available. This angle is at its strongest with
nothing around it.

**The rule that catches the failure:** the claim piece must be publishable to a reader who
has never seen the source and never will. Nothing in it refers to the source existing.

---

### 2. The counter-example

**Asserts:** the case that runs the other way, and why the claim survives it, or why the
claim needs narrowing.

**Needs from the source:** an exception, an edge case, a time it did not work, or a
situation the user would name as the boundary of their own position. If the source contains
no exception, do not invent one. Ask the user for one or skip this angle.

**Failure mode:** the fake counter-example, which is a straw case constructed so the claim
knocks it down. Readers detect this instantly and it costs more credibility than the piece
earns.

**Natural formats:** short post, or the opening of a thread that then rebuilds the claim.

**Why this angle is valuable:** it is the one that makes a reader trust the other six. A
person who publishes the boundary of their own position is read differently from a person
who publishes only the position.

---

### 3. The how

**Asserts:** the procedure. What someone should actually do, in order.

**Needs from the source:** a repeatable sequence. Steps, checks, a decision order, or a set
of criteria. A source that only states a conclusion cannot produce this angle honestly.

**Failure mode:** the how with no steps, which is the claim wearing a numbered list. If the
"steps" are "1. Care about ownership 2. Assign an owner 3. Hold them to it", there is no
procedure and this angle is not available from this source.

**Natural formats:** carousel or slide outline, because one point per slide is structurally
a procedure (`research/distilled-repurposing-and-format-adaptation.md`, section 2). Also the
thread.

**Budget note:** at a maximum of 60 words per slide across a recommended 5 to 15 slides, a
carousel is a 300 to 900 word artifact (same source, section 2). A how that does not fit
that budget is two carousels or one email.

---

### 4. The story behind it

**Asserts:** what actually happened. One situation, one sequence, one outcome.

**Needs from the source:** a specific incident with a time, a place or a role, and a
resolution. Generic composites do not qualify. If the source's story involves a named client
or an identifiable situation, this angle goes through the confidentiality screen before it
goes anywhere else.

**Failure mode:** two of them.

- **The story with no stakes.** Something happened and then it was fine. If the user was not
  wrong, worried, or surprised at some point in it, it is an anecdote, not a story.
- **The inflated story.** Turning a plain incident into dramatic prose. The sibling skill's
  guide has the measurement behind this: perceiving material as cheesy or oversentimental
  measurably reduces its own effect. See
  `../../said-it-already/references/spoken-to-written.md`.

**Natural formats:** email, which is the surface that tolerates length and a slow start
better than any feed. Also a long-form post.

**Confidentiality note:** this angle carries the highest do-not-publish rate in a typical
pack. Screen it first, not last.

---

### 5. The objection

**Asserts:** the strongest thing someone says against the claim, and the answer.

**Needs from the source:** an actual objection the user has heard. From a call, a comment, a
reply, a client. Not a hypothetical the user constructs.

**Failure mode:** answering a weak objection. The value of this angle is entirely in
choosing the objection that is genuinely hard. If the answer is obvious, the piece is
condescending to anyone who holds the objection, which includes a chunk of the audience.

**Natural formats:** short post, because the objection and the answer are two beats and two
beats is a short post. Also strong as a thread when the answer has conditions.

**The test:** the objection has to be quotable back at the user by someone who disagrees
with them, and still be the objection they wrote. If the user would not recognize it as what
their critics actually say, rewrite it.

---

### 6. The specific number

**Asserts:** one figure and what it means.

**Needs from the source:** a real number the user can stand behind. A duration, a count, a
cost, a rate, a percentage with a denominator.

**Failure mode:** three of them, and this angle fails more often than the rest.

- **Manufacturing the number.** If the source did not contain it, it does not exist. Under
  the evidence standards an unretrieved figure is a gap, not a claim
  (`evidence-standards.md`, rule 9).
- **Dropping the hedge.** "Roughly forty percent" cannot become "40%". This is a scrub item
  in the sibling skill's confidentiality screen and it applies identically here. See
  `../../said-it-already/references/confidentiality-screen.md`.
- **Publishing a number that belongs to someone else.** A client's revenue, margin, or
  headcount is theirs. The user's own result from an engagement may be publishable. The
  client's underlying figures are not. Same guide.

**Natural formats:** the shortest surface. A number with one sentence around it.

**Note on the carousel cover slide:** practice guidance says a cover slide should carry a
bold claim or a data point rather than a title
(`research/distilled-repurposing-and-format-adaptation.md`, section 2). If the pack has a
number angle and a carousel, the number frequently belongs on the cover.

---

### 7. The contrarian read

**Asserts:** where consensus in this space is wrong, and what the user thinks instead.

**Needs from the source:** a position the user holds that most people in their field do not.
The user has to actually hold it. Manufactured contrarianism is the most visible failure in
the whole taxonomy because everyone has seen it a thousand times.

**Failure mode:** two.

- **Contrarian about nothing.** Disagreeing with a position nobody holds. "Everyone thinks
  you should never talk to customers" is not a real consensus to break.
- **Contrarian as bait.** A take the user does not hold, published because it will get
  argued with. This one poisons the well for the other six pieces and it is the reason a
  reader stops trusting an account.

**Natural formats:** short post or the opening of a thread. This angle is at its worst
padded.

**The test before drafting:** ask the user directly whether they would defend this position
in a room full of people in their field who disagree. If the answer is anything short of
yes, cut the angle from the pack.

## Choosing angles for a pack

### The pack is 8 to 10 pieces and there are 7 angles

A pack is 5 to 7 short posts, one carousel outline, one email, one thread, and optionally a
short-video script or hook list. The angles do not map one to one, and they should not.

**Rules for the assignment.**

1. **Every angle appears at most twice in a pack, and a repeated angle must be on different
   surfaces with different supporting material.** Two claim pieces on the same surface is
   the failure this skill exists to catch.
2. **The claim angle appears exactly once.** It is the anchor. Two claim pieces is one claim
   piece and one echo.
3. **A source that supports fewer than four distinct angles is a weak source.** Say so
   rather than padding to seven. See `source-selection.md`.
4. **Assign the angle first, then the format.** The how goes to the carousel because a
   procedure is a slide sequence, not because the carousel slot needed filling.
5. **Do not force all seven.** A source with no number in it does not get a number angle.
   Reporting six real angles beats reporting seven with one invented.

### The assignment table the skill fills in

Every content pack carries this table, and it is what makes the variety auditable rather
than asserted:

| Piece | Surface | Angle | Claim sentence | Passes disagreement test against |
|---|---|---|---|---|

The last column names at least one other piece in the pack that a reader could disagree with
while agreeing with this one. If a piece cannot name one, it does not ship.

## What varying only depth does not fix

One practice source proposes allocating depth rather than duplicating meaning, giving each
surface a different amount of the reasoning
(`research/distilled-repurposing-and-format-adaptation.md`, section 5). That is a real and
separate planning axis, and it is not a substitute for the angle.

Angle decides WHAT CLAIM a piece makes. Depth decides how much reasoning ships with it. A
pack that varies only depth publishes the same claim at five lengths, which fails the
disagreement test on every pair.

Use depth as a second-order control after the angles are assigned.

---

## What varying only cadence does not fix

Standard practice prescribes spacing repurposed content across time and varying formats to
prevent repetition (`research/distilled-repurposing-and-format-adaptation.md`, section 4).

**That is a scheduling remedy for an argument problem.** Spacing five restatements over two
weeks makes the repetition slower. It does not make it smaller. The reader who follows the
user on three surfaces still gets the same claim three times, just further apart.

Sequencing is worth doing for its own reasons, covered in `sequencing.md`. It is not a
substitute for the angles either.
