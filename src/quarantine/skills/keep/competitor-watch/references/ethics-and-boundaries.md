# Ethics and boundaries

This skill derives competitive intelligence partly from content that was on the user's
screen because somebody else put it there: a post in a feed, a screenshot a friend shared,
a partner's screen during a demo, a slide left up on a client call. That is a real ethical
question and it deserves a real answer rather than a warning label.

Domain claims trace to `references/research/distilled-competitive-intelligence.md`.

## The professional standard, as it actually reads

The SCIP Code of Ethics has seven provisions. The three that bear on this skill, verbatim
(distillation section 7.1):

- "To comply with all applicable laws, domestic and international"
- "To accurately disclose all relevant information, including one's identity and
  organization, prior to all interviews"
- "To faithfully adhere to and abide by one's company policies, objectives and guidelines"

SCIP states the code "is not a corporate policy; it contains guidelines by which companies
and practitioners can set their own standards along the ethical spectrum" (distillation
section 7.1). It is a floor, and it explicitly pushes residual judgment back to the
practitioner.

## What the law prohibits

The Economic Espionage Act of 1996 criminalizes trade secret theft via bribery, hacking, or
misappropriation. Public-domain information triggers no liability; stolen trade secrets
trigger criminal exposure (distillation section 7.2). Alongside it: the Uniform Trade
Secrets Act, antitrust rules against competitors discussing pricing or market division,
fraud statutes covering misrepresentation of identity or purpose, and ordinary law on
trespass and theft (distillation section 7.2).

## The prohibited list

Consolidated from the archive (distillation section 7.3):

- Hacking or credential reuse for system access
- Misrepresenting identity, employer, or purpose, including for gated content
- Theft of documents, prototypes, or trade secrets
- Paying insiders for confidential information
- Unlawful conversation recording
- Pretending to be a customer
- Pressuring anyone to disclose information covered by an NDA

## The pattern across every enforcement case

Air Canada v. WestJet, Volkswagen and GM, HP, Oracle and Microsoft, Uber and Waymo, Tesla
and XPeng, Apple and Baidu (distillation section 7.5). Settlements from 14.5 million to 179
million dollars, plus a prison sentence.

**In every one of them, a person took, accessed, or bought material they were not entitled
to have.** Not one case involves someone drawing a conclusion from something they
legitimately saw (distillation section 7.5).

That pattern is the hinge of this entire guide. The prohibitions are about **acquisition
conduct**. They are not about the act of noticing.

## The gap, stated honestly

**No source in this archive gives a rule for information that arrives without being sought:
material observed during a partner or client screen share, a screenshot someone else
posted, a slide left up on a call, a competitor's dashboard visible in a demo**
(distillation section 7.7).

The SCIP disclosure provision governs interviews, which is active elicitation. Every
prohibited practice in the list is an act of taking, accessing, or misrepresenting. The one
article in the archive specifically about competitive intelligence going too far gives no
handling rule for inadvertent receipt (distillation section 7.7).

So the rule below is constructed by analogy from four things the archive does establish,
and it is labeled as inference rather than presented as a sourced professional standard
(distillation section 7.7):

1. The prohibitions target acquisition conduct, not observation.
2. NDAs bind across changes of context, as with an employee arriving from a competitor.
3. Certain categories are named as more protected regardless of how they were obtained:
   pricing data, salesforce compensation, development and marketing timelines, bundling
   strategies.
4. The working publication test is whether you would want it in the news.

## The rule

### 1. Noticing is not collecting

If it was legitimately in front of you in the ordinary course of business, forming a view
about your market from it is normal competitive awareness. That sits on the low-risk
internal-company-sources rung of the practitioner risk ladder, which runs secondary
research (lowest), internal sources (low), external informant networks (moderate), trade
show intelligence (moderate to high), direct primary elicitation (highest) (distillation
section 7.4).

A competitor's logo on a slide in a prospect's deck, a rival tool named on a call, a
pricing page a friend screenshotted into a group chat: these are things you saw. Using them
to understand your market is what every practitioner in the archive calls field
intelligence, and it is the source category the literature says companies underuse
(distillation section 2.1).

### 2. Deliberately mining another party's screen is collecting

Do not do any of the following, and do not ask this skill to:

- Scan a partner's or client's screen share for material unrelated to the meeting's purpose
- Extract their customer lists, internal dashboards, financials, roadmaps, or credentials
- Reconstruct a document from a screen share that they showed briefly and did not share
- Retrieve captures of a specific person's screen share for the purpose of learning their
  confidential business
- Search the record with queries designed to surface a named partner's internal data

The distinction is intent and target. Noticing a competitor's name on a shared screen is
awareness. Running a retrieval pass whose object is a partner's internal information is
acquisition, and it belongs with the prohibited conduct.

**Operational form of this rule for retrieval:** every query this skill runs is aimed at a
watchlist entity, a category shape, or a market topic. No query is ever aimed at a person
or company for the purpose of extracting their internal business. If a retrieval pass is
returning a partner's internal material, the query is wrong. Narrow it or drop it.

### 3. NDA and confidence material stays out entirely

Anything shared under an NDA, anything a person prefaced with a request to keep it between
you, and anything from a confidential engagement does not enter the sightings log, the
digest, or the working notes. Not summarized, not paraphrased, not "informing the
analysis". NDAs bind across context changes (distillation section 7.7, point 2).

If the user is under an NDA with a party who is also on the watchlist, ask before the first
run how they want that handled, and default to excluding that party's material entirely.

### 4. Protected categories stay internal even when legitimately seen

Pricing data, compensation, development and marketing timelines, and bundling strategies
are named as more protected than ordinary public information (distillation section 7.3).
When this skill surfaces one of these from an internal sighting rather than a public
source, it may appear in the user's own digest. It does not get repeated outward in any
form.

### 5. Third-party content is theirs, not yours

**State this plainly, because it is the rule most likely to be broken by accident.**

Screen capture of another person's post, dashboard, screen share, or message is competitive
input. The intelligence derived from it stays internal to the user.

Nothing captured from another person's screen gets:

- Republished, in whole or in part
- Quoted publicly, including in marketing, social posts, sales collateral, or a battlecard
  that leaves the company
- Shared outward to anyone outside the user's own organization
- Attributed publicly to the person whose screen it came from
- Screenshotted, exported, or attached to anything that goes to a third party

The digest is an internal working document. Treat it that way. The publication test from
the archive is the check: would you want this in the news (distillation section 7.4). If the
answer involves explaining how you came to have somebody else's screen contents, the answer
is no.

This is also the repo standard independently: raw capture never ships, and nothing derived
from another person's private messages, another company's dashboard, or a screen share ends
up in a committed file or a shared artifact (`references/evidence-standards.md`, rule 7).

### 6. People in the capture are incidental

Other people appear in the user's sightings because they were in the room. Include a person
only where they are material to a tracked entity (a competitor's founder posting about
their launch is material; a friend who happened to share the screenshot is not). Do not
build a profile of anyone. Sensitive categories stay out entirely
(`references/evidence-standards.md`, rule 10).

Name the source person in a receipt only when the identity of the source is what makes the
sighting meaningful, for example a client naming a competitor on a call. Otherwise the
receipt records the app and the context type, not the individual.

### 7. Liability follows the agent

"Your vendor must follow the same laws as your company", and companies remain liable when
vendors are pushed to violate law. Hired investigators "operate under your name, and their
illegal activities become your headlines" (distillation section 7.8).

An automated agent collecting on the user's behalf sits inside that principle. This skill
does not get a lower standard because it is software. Every rule above applies to the
skill's own retrieval and to anything it drafts.

## The two-question test

When something in the record is ambiguous, run these in order:

1. **How did this get in front of me?** Ordinary course of business, or an act of getting
   at something? If the second, stop.
2. **Would I be comfortable explaining how I know this, to the person whose screen it came
   from?** If not, it stays out of the digest.

The archive's own heuristics say the same thing in blunter terms: "If it feels wrong, it
probably is" and "If you don't want your mother to see it in the news, you probably
shouldn't do it" (distillation section 7.4).

## Note on a conflict in the archive

One source argues for eliminating gray areas entirely: "if you find yourself asking whether
a practice is legal, then you probably shouldn't be doing it" (distillation section 7.6).
Another publishes a five-rung risk ladder and explains how to operate safely on each rung
(distillation section 7.6).

This skill follows the graded ladder and keeps the bright-line rule as a tie-breaker. The
reason: the bright-line version as written would forbid ordinary market research, and the
same source that states it also endorses interviewing industry professionals as a "normal
and natural part of trading" (distillation section 7.6). Where the ladder does not settle a
case, apply the bright line and leave it out.

## What this skill will refuse

Say so plainly and stop, rather than producing a degraded version:

- A request to extract a specific partner's, client's, or vendor's confidential business
  from screen-share captures
- A request to reconstruct a document or dashboard belonging to another party
- A request to publish, quote, or send outward anything derived from another person's
  screen
- A request to use NDA-covered material in any part of the analysis
- A request to draft outreach that reveals knowledge the user could only have from someone
  else's screen
