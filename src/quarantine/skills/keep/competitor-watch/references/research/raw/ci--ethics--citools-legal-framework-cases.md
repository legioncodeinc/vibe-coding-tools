## SCIP code restated as seven commitments

1. Increase recognition and respect for the profession
2. "Comply with all applicable laws, domestic and international"
3. "Accurately disclose all relevant information, including one's identity and
   organization, before every interview"
4. Avoid conflicts of interest
5. Provide honest recommendations and conclusions
6. Promote the code within company, contractors, and profession
7. Adhere to company policies and guidelines

Stated enforcement mechanics: the disclosure clause eliminates pretexting; the
recommendations clause covers analytical integrity.

## Legal framework

- **Primary statute:** Economic Espionage Act of 1996, 18 U.S.C. sections 1831 to 1839,
  criminalizing trade secret theft via bribery, hacking, or misappropriation.
- **Critical distinction:** information from the public domain triggers no liability;
  stolen trade secrets trigger criminal exposure.
- **Secondary legislation:** Telephone Records and Privacy Protection Act of 2006, passed
  after the HP case, which made pretexting for phone records a federal felony.

## Ethical sources, as listed

- Competitor websites, pricing pages, job postings
- Regulatory filings, patents, earnings calls
- Trade shows and conferences, with full disclosure of identity and employer
- Product teardowns of purchased items
- Review platforms, news, conference talks

## Prohibited practices, as listed

- Hacking or credential reuse for system access
- Misrepresenting identity, employer, or purpose
- Theft of documents, prototypes, trade secrets
- Paying insiders for confidential information
- Unlawful conversation recording

## Named cases

1. **Air Canada v. WestJet (2004 to 2006).** WestJet managers used a former employee's
   credentials to access a password-protected website. Settled at 15.5 million dollars.
2. **Volkswagen and GM (1997).** VW paid 100 million dollars over documents illegally
   obtained from a departing executive.
3. **HP (2006).** Pretexting phone carriers for the records of 12 people. Settled at 14.5
   million dollars.
4. **Oracle and Microsoft (2000).** Hired investigators sought the trash of
   Microsoft-aligned groups; exposed in the press.

## Extension to third parties

The code extends beyond employees to contractors and agencies: "hired private
investigators operate under your name, and their illegal activities become your
headlines." Programs should train external firms on ethics standards.
