# False Alerts in Air Traffic Control Conflict Alerting System: Is There a "Cry Wolf" Effect?

- **URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720809344720
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Citation:** Wickens, C.D., Rice, S., Keller, D., Hutchins, S., Hughes, J., and Clayton,
  K. (2009). Human Factors, 51(4), 446 to 462.
- **Source type:** academic (peer reviewed, Human Factors)
- **Why archived:** The counterexample. Archived specifically because it disagrees with the
  clinical alert fatigue literature, and the contract requires conflicts to be reported
  rather than smoothed. Well outside the 12-month window and used deliberately as the
  canonical negative result on the cry wolf hypothesis.

## Question

Whether high false-alarm rates in midair conflict alerts cause air traffic controllers to
ignore true alerts or delay responding to them, which is the cry wolf phenomenon.

## Results

**False alert rate:** "Forty-five percent of the alerts were false, ranging from 0.28 to
0.58."

**Effect on response:** centers with more false alerts showed more nonresponses overall,
but the investigators found "no evidence that these were nonresponses to true alerts or
that response times were delayed in those centers."

**Controller behavior:** rather than cry wolf behavior, controllers showed "desirable
anticipatory behavior by issuing trajectory changes prior to the alert." The alert was
frequently redundant because the expert had already acted.

**Trust:** controllers showed "more reliance on, and less compliance with, the alerting
system" for conflicts that were difficult to visualize.

## Conclusion

Quoted: "The high false-alarm rate does not appear to induce cry wolf behavior in the
context of en route ATC conflict alerts." The authors saw no need for substantial algorithm
modification, though they suggested refinement for conflicts that are hard to visualize.

## Why this matters to the archive

A 45% false alert rate did not degrade expert response in this setting. Compare the
clinical finding of a 92.9% override rate under a 92.7% inappropriate alert rate
[routine--alert-fatigue--jmir-alert-appropriateness-2022.md]. The two results are not
directly comparable, and the differences (expertise, stakes, visibility of the underlying
condition, whether the operator could already see the problem independently) are exactly
what a rubric has to reason about.
