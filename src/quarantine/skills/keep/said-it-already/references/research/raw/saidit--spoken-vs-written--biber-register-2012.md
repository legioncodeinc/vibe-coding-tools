# Register as a Predictor of Linguistic Variation

- **Title:** Register as a predictor of linguistic variation
- **Author:** Douglas Biber
- **URL:** https://jan.ucc.nau.edu/biber/Biber/Biber_2012.pdf
- **Published in:** Corpus Linguistics and Linguistic Theory 8-1 (2012), pages 9 to 37
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic

## Window note

This source is from 2012, outside the default 6-month research window. It is archived
anyway because it is the foundational corpus-linguistic measurement of how spoken and
written language actually differ, and nothing published in the last 6 months replaces it.
Corpus register findings are stable literature, not news.

## Extracted content

**Definition of register.** Biber treats register as contextual variation: the same
speaker produces systematically different linguistic patterns in different communicative
situations. He argues that "descriptions of the average patterns of use in a general
corpus do not accurately describe any register."

**The core claim.** Situational context is the primary driver of linguistic form. In his
words, "the patterns of use in speech are dramatically different from the patterns in
writing (especially academic writing)."

**Pronouns.** In the spoken London-Lund Corpus, "the pronouns I and you are among the
most frequent words." They are "considerably less frequent" in the written Brown Corpus.

**Formulaic sequences.** Conversation runs on bundles like "I don't know if", "I don't
know what", "do you want to". Written academic prose runs on "on the other hand", "in the
case of", "one of the most". Of the 133 four-word lexical bundles occurring above 20
times per million words, only four overlapped between conversation and academic prose,
roughly a 3% overlap.

**Clause architecture.** Biber describes a fundamental opposition: "verbs and finite
dependent clauses co-occurring frequently with pronouns and adverbials in spoken
registers, versus nouns, attributive adjectives, and prepositional phrases" in academic
writing.

**Passive voice.** Passive verbs occur roughly 2,000 times per million words in
conversation against roughly 18,000 times per million words in academic writing.

**The that-complementizer.** "When a that-clause is used in academic prose, it will
almost always retain the that complementizer. When a that-clause is used in conversation,
it will usually omit the complementizer."

**Methodological advice.** "The practice advocated here is to begin a research study with
the hypothesis that such register differences exist, and to include analysis of those
differences unless they are empirically shown to be unimportant."

## Claims this source supports

1. Spoken register and written register are measurably different systems, not the same
   language at different tidiness levels. The 3% lexical bundle overlap is the sharpest
   single number for this.
2. A transcript line moved into a post carries spoken-register markers (first and second
   person pronouns, verb-heavy clause chains, dropped complementizers) that a reader
   registers as unedited speech.
3. The fix is not "clean up the grammar". The fix is re-encoding the idea in the target
   register, because the two registers select different structures for the same meaning.
4. Written prose is noun-heavy and clause-light where speech is verb-heavy and
   clause-chained. Any spoken-to-written rebuild is a structural conversion.
