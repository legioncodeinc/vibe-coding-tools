## Extracted content

**Definition.** Green and Brock (2000), quoted in this chapter, define transportation as
"an experiential state of immersion in which all mental processes are concentrated on the
events occurring in the narrative." Transported readers "respond emotionally to narrative
events and form vivid mental images of narrative settings and characters."

**Meta-analytic evidence.**

| Meta-analysis | Finding as reported in this chapter |
|---|---|
| Van Laer et al. (2014), 76 articles | "individuals who are more transported into narratives show more narrative-consistent affect, beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors across a range of topics" |
| Oschatz and Marker (2020) | "narratives are more effective than non-narratives at creating change over time for attitudes, intentions, and behaviors" |
| Shen et al. (2015), health narratives | "narratives had a small effect on persuasion, and the effects were stronger for audio or video narratives" |

**What raises transportation.**

- Character identification. Cohen (2001) defines identification as "a process that
  consists of loss of self-awareness and its temporary replacement with heightened
  emotional and cognitive connections with a character."
- Emotional content. De Graaf et al. (2016) found "stories with high emotional content
  were more likely to have effects."
- Emotional flow, meaning shifts in emotion across the story (Nabi and Green, 2015).
- Vivid mental imagery. The Transportation-Imagery Model holds that "transportation can
  evoke vivid images, and that this story-linked mental imagery is an important driver of
  belief change."
- Narrativity, meaning the story actually has story structure.
- Perceived realism, which is psychological rather than factual. Readers are transported
  by stories "that are not factual or typical."

**What lowers transportation.**

- Scrambled order. "The random re-arrangement of story parts decreased transportation"
  (Appel et al., 2015).
- Instructing readers to evaluate the text critically while reading reduced
  transportation (Green and Brock, 2000).
- Cognitive load and distraction during reading (Hamby et al., 2017).
- Realism violations, such as anachronistic detail (Busselle and Vierrether, 2022).
- Affective resistance. Appel (2022) found that participants who perceived narrative ads
  as "cheesy or oversentimental" experienced lower transportation.

## Claims this source supports

1. Narrative persuades, and the mechanism is measured, not folklore. This is the
   strongest evidence in this archive for the client-story seed type.
2. Effect sizes are described as small in at least one health meta-analysis. Storytelling
   is not a magic multiplier and should not be sold as one.
3. Order matters. A story told out of sequence loses its effect, so the rebuild step has
   to impose sequence on a rambling spoken account.
4. Specificity and vivid concrete detail drive the imagery that drives belief change.
   This is a direct argument for keeping the real numbers, names of things, and physical
   details from the original spoken moment.
5. Overcooked emotional writing backfires. "Cheesy or oversentimental" reduces the effect.
   That is an evidenced argument against inflating a plain spoken line into dramatic prose.
