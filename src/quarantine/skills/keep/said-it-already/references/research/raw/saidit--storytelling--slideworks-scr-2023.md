## Source-quality caveat

The article attributes SCR to McKinsey and does NOT attribute it to Barbara Minto or the
Pyramid Principle. A second source consulted in this sweep (stratechi.com) also declines
to name an originator. **This archive therefore cannot establish who invented SCR.** The
structure is used and described consistently across sources; its provenance is a gap.

## Extracted content

**The three elements, verbatim from the source:**

| Element | Definition |
|---|---|
| Situation | "Fact-based description of the current situation." |
| Complication | "The reason the situation requires action. What is the problem (or opportunity)?" |
| Resolution | "What we need to do to resolve this complication (or capture the opportunity)?" |

**Worked example (cybersecurity):**

- Situation: "The Enterprise Cyber Security market in the US is rapidly growing at a 20%
  CAGR."
- Complication: "Norton is poorly positioned to get a share in this growing profit pool."
- Resolution: "Norton must acquire a leading competitor and invest $200 million in
  building capabilities."

**Proportion guidance.** Keep "the Situation and Complication sections as clear and short
as possible" because "your audience is more interested in solutions." The Resolution
carries most of the weight.

**Reordering.** An alternative RSC ordering puts the Resolution first when the audience
is already predisposed to agree.

## Claims this source supports

1. A three-beat business story structure (setup, disruption, response) is standard
   practice and can be applied to a spoken client story that arrived out of order.
2. The setup should be short. Most spoken client stories over-invest in setup because the
   listener needed context that a reader can be given in one line.
3. Front-loading the resolution is a legitimate variant when the audience already agrees,
   which maps onto short-form social posts that lead with the outcome.
4. Provenance gap: this archive cannot say who created SCR.
