## Source-quality caveat

This is a secondhand compilation. The primary sources it cites (the Oldroyd MIT and
InsideSales work, the 2011 Harvard Business Review article by Oldroyd, McElheran and
Elkington) are paywalled or unreachable to direct fetch: HBR returned only headers and
metadata behind its paywall on 2026-08-17. The figures below are therefore recorded as
"as cited by," not as verified primary findings. This is a named gap.

## Extracted content

**InsideSales.com and MIT study, as cited**

- "the odds of qualifying a lead are 21 times higher if you contact them within 5 minutes
  versus 30 minutes"
- "After 5 minutes, the odds of successfully contacting a lead drop by 10 times"
- "35 to 50% of all sales are won by the vendor that responds first"

**Harvard Business Review study, as cited**

- Leads contacted within an hour are "7 times more likely to have meaningful conversations
  with decision-makers" than those contacted later.
- Authors of the 2011 HBR piece, confirmed from the article header directly:
  James B. Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran, and David Elkington. Industries studied:
  financial services, automobiles, education, software, health care, professional
  services.

## Claims this source supports

1. The "5 minutes versus 30 minutes, 21x" figure is the canonical speed-to-lead number
   and traces to InsideSales and MIT work, as cited secondhand.
2. The direction of every cited study agrees: faster wins, and the advantage is large.
   The magnitudes disagree substantially across sources (21x, 391%, 8x, 7x), which is
   what you would expect from different channels, definitions, and vintages. Report the
   direction with confidence and the magnitude with a range.
3. All figures here describe outbound calling on form-fill leads. None describe replying
   to a social hand-raiser. Same caveat as
   `leadharvest--speed-to-lead--artemis-benchmark-2026.md`.
