## What it says

On the written-agreement requirement, verbatim: "Always specify your late fee terms in
writing before extending credit." Without written terms, a creditor is limited to state
default rates, which the page describes as typically much lower than contractual rates.

On typical commercial rates, verbatim: "commercial late fees of 1.5% to 2% per month
(18-24% annualized) are widely enforced across most states."

State examples given:

| State | As printed |
|---|---|
| California | 10% per annum default; no cap on contracts over $300,000 |
| Texas | No usury cap for commercial transactions |
| New York | 9% statutory default; up to 24% enforced in commercial contexts |
| Florida | 18% annually for amounts under $500,000 |
| Construction-sector states | Prompt payment acts mandate minimum interest, 1% to 2% monthly |

On the consumer and commercial split, verbatim: "The legal treatment of late fees differs
dramatically between commercial and consumer contexts." Consumer fees are heavily
regulated; commercial fees receive more deference to agreed terms between sophisticated
parties.

On penalties versus liquidated damages, verbatim: "Your late fee should reasonably
approximate the cost of late payment, not punish the debtor."

## Limitations noted at fetch time

A vendor blog is not legal authority on usury caps, and usury law is state-specific,
transaction-specific, and changes. The rate figures here are directional only.
