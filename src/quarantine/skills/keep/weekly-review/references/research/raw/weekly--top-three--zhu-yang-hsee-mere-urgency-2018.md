# The Mere Urgency Effect

- **URL:** https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/45/3/673/4847790
- **Also indexed at:** https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2019-23349-011
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic (Zhu, Yang, Hsee; Journal of Consumer Research, volume 45,
  issue 3, October 2018). Abstract-level retrieval only.

## The effect

"a tendency to pursue urgency over importance even when these normative reasons are
controlled for."

## Core experimental finding, verbatim

"people are more likely to perform unimportant tasks (i.e., tasks with objectively lower
payoffs) over important tasks (i.e., tasks with objectively better payoffs), when the
unimportant tasks are characterized merely by spurious urgency."

Five experiments. The normative explanations that would ordinarily justify picking the
urgent task, task difficulty, completion windows, and immediate payoffs, were controlled
for, which is what makes the effect "mere".

## Why it matters

The behavior violates dominance: "choosing objectively worse options over objectively better
options". And "pursuing an urgent task has its own appeal, independent of its objective
consequence."

## Reading for skill design

This is the sourced reason a top three cannot be the three loudest items.

Consequences encoded in the selection method:

1. **Urgency is discounted, not weighted equally.** Left to itself, a person already
   over-weights it. A selection method that scores urgency alongside consequence at equal
   weight reproduces the bias with a spreadsheet attached.
2. **Spurious urgency is filtered before scoring.** Urgency counts only when it comes from a
   real date attached to a real source. Sender adjectives, a vendor's "ASAP", and a countdown
   in a marketing email are the exact stimulus this paper manipulated.
3. **Consequence must be stated in the output.** If the reasoning shown to the user names
   only the deadline, the user is being handed the bias back.

## Caveats

- Abstract-level retrieval. Effect sizes, sample sizes and moderators not read.
- Consumer-behavior laboratory tasks. Transfer to a professional weekly planning decision is
  plausible but untested in anything this archive retrieved.
- The abstract as retrieved does not confirm the perceived-self-importance moderator that
  secondary coverage attributes to this paper. Do not cite that moderator.
