## Definition and purpose

A scoping review provides "an overview of the available research evidence" rather than
answering a specific question.

Four reasons the guide gives for running one:

1. Map the scope of research on a topic.
2. Evaluate the need for a systematic review.
3. Summarize existing evidence.
4. Identify research gaps.

## Scoping review versus systematic review

The distinction the guide draws: a scoping review does not aim "to answer a specific
question to guide decision-making," whereas a systematic review does.

Quoting Tricco et al., the PRISMA extension for scoping reviews: scoping reviews "are
generally conducted to provide an overview of the existing evidence regardless of
methodological quality or risk of bias."

## Quality appraisal

Scoping reviews do "not typically include a risk of bias assessment." Included sources
"are typically not critically appraised for scoping reviews" and scoping reviews "often
prioritize breadth over depth."

## Standards named

- Conduct: JBI Scoping Review Guidance.
- Reporting: PRISMA-ScR (PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews).
- Foundational method: Arksey and O'Malley (2005), with later refinements.

## Why this source is in the archive

It supplies the honest label for what a practitioner topic synthesis actually is. A topic
question answered once, broadly, to map what exists and find the gaps, without pooling
effects and without formal risk-of-bias scoring, is a scoping exercise. Calling it a
systematic review would overclaim. The scoping review literature also makes the tradeoff
explicit: breadth over depth, and no quality appraisal built in, which means quality has to
be handled some other way if the output is going to be acted on.
