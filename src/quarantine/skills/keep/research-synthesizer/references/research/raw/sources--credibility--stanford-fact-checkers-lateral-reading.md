# Fact checkers outperform historians when evaluating online information

- **URL:** https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2017/10/fact-checkers-outperform-historians-evaluating-online-information
- **Publisher:** Stanford Report, reporting Stanford History Education Group research
- **Underlying paper:** Wineburg, S. and McGrew, S., "Lateral Reading: Reading Less and Learning More When Evaluating Digital Information", October 2017
- **Published:** October 2017
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic
- **Note on quotes:** dashes inside quoted passages normalized to spaced hyphens.

## Window exception

Outside the six-month window. This is the founding study for lateral reading and is still
the standard citation. Recorded as a deliberate exception.

## Study design

Three groups compared on the same credibility tasks:

- 10 professional fact checkers from news outlets
- 10 PhD historians holding full-time faculty positions
- 25 Stanford undergraduates

Tasks included judging the reliability of two similarly named organizations writing about
bullying (the American Academy of Pediatrics against the American College of
Pediatricians), and investigating who funded the Vergara v. California lawsuit.

## Findings

Fact checkers were "fastest and most accurate". Historians and students were readily
deceived.

- Students overwhelmingly judged the advocacy group's site the more reliable of the two.
- Historians largely accepted both pediatric organizations as credible.

## The mechanism: lateral versus vertical reading

- **Lateral reading.** Fact checkers scanned a site briefly, then **left it** and opened
  other tabs to find out what the rest of the web said about that source before evaluating
  its claims.
- **Vertical reading.** Historians and students stayed on the page and evaluated the source
  using the page's own signals. They were misled by professional appearance, logos, and
  nonprofit status.

## Quote

Sam Wineburg: "Very intelligent people were bamboozled by the ruses that are part of the
toolkit of digital deception."

## Why this source is in the archive

It establishes the single most transferable rule for grading sources at speed: **credibility
is determined off the page, not on it.** Expertise in the subject matter does not substitute
for it. The historians in this study were domain experts and still lost to fact checkers
who left the page first.
