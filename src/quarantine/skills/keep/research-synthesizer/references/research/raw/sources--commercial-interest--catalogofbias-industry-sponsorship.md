## Definition

Industry sponsorship bias: "A tendency for the methods and results of a study to support
the interests of the funding organisation."

## The mechanisms named

The entry lists how sponsored work becomes skewed, and the list matters more than the
headline because each item is a thing a reader can look for:

- Designing research questions to yield misleading answers
- Selecting unrepresentative populations
- Administering competitor products at suboptimal doses
- Questionable analytical choices
- Suppressing statistically nonsignificant findings
- Selective outcome reporting
- Republishing positive results multiple times

## The quantitative evidence

**Lundh et al., Cochrane methodology review "Industry sponsorship and research outcome",
Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2017 Feb 16;2:MR000033.**

| Measure | Result |
|---|---|
| Efficacy results favouring the sponsor's product | Risk ratio 1.27 (95% CI 1.17 to 1.37) |
| Authors' conclusions favouring the sponsor's product | Risk ratio 1.34 (95% CI 1.19 to 1.51) |
| Effect on harm estimates | No effect found; evidence quality very low |

The conclusions figure exceeding the results figure is the finding to carry forward:
**sponsored work is skewed more in what it concludes than in what it measures.**

**Bero 2007, statin trials.** Among 65 trials sponsored by makers of newer drugs,
"favourable results were reported in 66% of the studies and favourable conclusions drawn in
79%." Where an older drug's maker sponsored the trial, only 3 of 30 studies (10 percent)
reported favourable results for the newer statins and 4 (13 percent) endorsed them in the
conclusions.

**Turner 2008, antidepressant publication bias.** "37 of 38 (97%) pre-market trials" with
FDA-positive results were published. "Of 36 trials with negative or questionable results,
22 (61%) were not published, 11 (31%) were published with narrative 'spin', and only 3 (8%)
were accurately represented as negative." Selective publication inflated published effect
sizes by roughly 30 percent against the FDA submissions.

**Barnes and Bero 1998, second-hand smoke reviews.** "Of authors with no ties to industry,
87% (65/75) found negative health effects; whereas, only 6% (2/31) of authors with industry
ties drew similar conclusions."

## Prevention and detection measures listed

1. Researchers retain control of design, conduct, analysis and reporting; avoid contracts
   with non-disclosure clauses or sponsor involvement in methodology and publication.
2. "The most effective preventative measure is likely a firewall, in which companies
   contribute to a general research fund but do not directly sponsor specific trials."
3. Design standards: best available comparator, equivalent dosing, non-pharmacological
   comparators where relevant, scientific rather than marketing purpose.
4. Transparency: preregistered detailed trial plans with planned secondary analyses
   specified, and public availability of all study data.

## Transfer caveat

All of this evidence is from clinical and pharmaceutical research. Applying the size of
these effects to vendor marketing content in a non-clinical field would be an unsupported
extrapolation. What transfers is the **direction and the mechanism**, particularly that
conclusions skew harder than results, and that non-publication of unfavourable findings is
a documented behaviour rather than a suspicion.
