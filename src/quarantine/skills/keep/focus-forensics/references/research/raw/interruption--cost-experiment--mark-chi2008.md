# The Cost of Interrupted Work: More Speed and Stress

- **Title:** The Cost of Interrupted Work: More Speed and Stress. Gloria Mark, Daniela Gudith, Ulrich Klocke. Proceedings of ACM CHI 2008.
- **URL:** http://www.ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic (peer-reviewed conference paper, controlled laboratory experiment)

## Why this source matters for focus-forensics

Two reasons. It is the paper most frequently cited as the source of the "23 minutes 15
seconds" figure, and this archive verified directly that the number 23 does not appear
anywhere in it. And its actual finding is the opposite of the folklore: interrupted
participants finished FASTER, and paid for it in stress rather than in time.

## Direct verification of the miscitation

The full text was fetched and searched. **The number 23 does not appear anywhere in the
document.** This is a first-hand check, not a repetition of the citation audit archived at
`myth--23-minutes-citation-audit--oberien-2023.md`, and it independently confirms that
audit's central claim.

## Method, quoted

- "Forty-eight subjects participated. 81% of subjects were German university students with
  a mean age of 26", "Fifteen were males and 33 were females."
- "A 3x2 factorial experimental design was used. The within-subject factor was interruption
  context with three levels: no interruption (B, baseline), same-context interruption (S),
  and different-context interruption (D)." Between-subjects factor was interruption medium,
  telephone or instant message.
- Task: role-play as an HR manager answering 12 emails per condition. "Subjects had just
  returned from vacation and were carefully instructed in all conditions (and were given
  incentives) to answer all emails in their inbox as quickly, correctly and politely as
  possible."
- "Interruption frequency was set to two minutes, based on observations from [6] and the
  pilot experiments."

## Findings, quoted

Time to complete the task, in minutes with standard deviation:

| Condition | Time |
|---|---|
| Baseline, no interruption | 22.77 (7.60) |
| Same-context interruption | 20.31 (5.94) |
| Different-context interruption | 20.60 (4.93) |

"Surprisingly, paired contrasts showed that subjects took the longest time in the baseline
condition to perform the task."

Workload measures, on a scale of 1 to 20:

| Measure | Baseline | Same-context | Different-context |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stress | 6.92 (3.85) | 9.46 (3.97) | 9.13 (4.10) |
| Frustration | 4.73 (2.93) | 6.63 (4.19) | 6.48 (4.45) |
| Time pressure | 11.02 (4.57) | 12.69 (4.45) | 12.17 (4.26) |
| Effort | 9.50 (3.38) | 11.04 (3.78) | 11.52 (3.31) |

Authors' conclusion: "When people are constantly interrupted, they develop a mode of
working faster (and writing less) to compensate for the time they know they will lose by
being interrupted. Yet working faster with interruptions has its cost: people in the
interrupted conditions experienced a higher workload, more stress, higher frustration,
more time pressure, and effort." And: "Context does not make a difference but surprisingly,
people completed interrupted tasks in less time with no difference in quality."

## Limitations, quoted

- "While laboratory studies are always subject to criticism of ecological validity, our
  task design was based on real fieldwork."
- "We cannot say whether people would cope over time or if these measures would only
  increase."

## Direct implication for the skill

1. **The cost of fragmentation in the best controlled evidence available is not measured in
   lost hours.** It showed up as stress, frustration, time pressure and effort, while
   elapsed time went down. A skill that reports fragmentation as an hours figure is
   reporting the one dimension this experiment found moving the wrong way.
2. **This directly licenses the skill's forbidden-claims list.** "You lost N hours to
   context switching" is not a conservative restatement of the literature. It contradicts
   the specific experiment most often cited in support of it.
3. **Interruptions in this study arrived every two minutes for a twenty minute task in a
   laboratory.** Nothing about the effect sizes transfers to a workweek, and the paper does
   not claim it does.
