## Why this source matters for focus-forensics

It is the most recent measurement of workplace multitasking structure in the archive, and
it disagrees with the twenty-year-old baseline in a way that the authors themselves refuse
to treat as a trend. That refusal is the model for how this skill handles comparison
across differently-measured windows.

## Method, quoted

- "Fifteen participants (12 male, two female, and one non-binary)", ages 21 to 54 (M =
  30.5, SD = 10.7).
- Each recorded "a pre-scheduled one-hour work session" wearing video-recording glasses,
  then self-annotated the recording in a lab session with experimenter support, using
  Atlas.ti for labeling. There is no automated sampling interval. The unit of observation
  is a human-labeled video segment.
- Activity taxonomy: main task, on-task supporting activity, off-task activity. The "main
  working sphere" combines main-task and on-task.

## Findings, quoted

| Measure | Value as printed |
|---|---|
| Main working sphere before switching off-task | "approximately six minutes (M = 364.46 sec, SD = 229.33 sec, Mdn = 300.72 sec)" |
| Main task alone before switching | "participants spent nearly 3.5 minutes on a main task before switching" |
| Off-task episode length | "M = 54 seconds, SD = 100 seconds" |
| Switches per one-hour session | "The mean number of switches per participant per working session was 36 (SD: 17)", median "29 times (IQR: 22.5) per session" |
| Share of session on main task | "61.63% [SD 17.79%]" |
| Share off-task | "21.82% [SD 16.38%]" |
| Share on-task supporting | "15.77% [SD 17.26%]" |
| Device split | computer "89.69% [SD 8.85%]", smartphone "3.63% [SD 3.89%]", analog "5.79% [SD 6.95%]" |
| Self-initiated share of off-task time | "Nearly 60% of off-task time" was self-initiated, versus 40 percent externally interrupted |

The study examined early versus late session time as a predictor and found only
personality correlations, not time-of-day patterns in switching. No meeting effects were
reported.

## Limitations, quoted

1. "With only 15 participants, broad generalization is limited."
2. "Short session time may not capture long-term multitasking patterns."
3. "As sessions were self-scheduled, results may not fully generalize to collaborative or
   dynamic tasks."
4. "Participants recorded their own work sessions, which could theoretically influence
   behavior."
5. "While we took several steps to ensure consistency in how participants labeled their
   activities...some variation is expected. As with any method involving self-assessment,
   subjective differences may affect how tasks were interpreted and coded."
6. "Any cross-study comparisons should be interpreted with care, as differences in methods,
   measures, and study contexts may limit direct comparability."

## The conflict, stated rather than smoothed

The 2005 stopwatch study reports roughly 11 minutes in a working sphere before a switch.
This 2025 video-annotation study reports roughly 6 minutes in a main working sphere. The
obvious reading, that focus has halved in twenty years, is the reading the popular press
takes. The authors of this paper explicitly decline it, in limitation 6.

The two studies differ in observer (external ethnographer versus the participant
themselves), in instrument (stopwatch and notepad versus video and retrospective labeling),
in unit (working sphere across a full workday versus main working sphere within a
scheduled one-hour session), in population, in sample size and in country. Any one of those
could produce the gap. This archive therefore records both numbers and records that the
difference between them is not interpretable.

## Direct implication for the skill

This is the whole argument for week-over-week reporting compressed into one example. Two
honest measurements of the same construct, taken by different methods, differ by a factor
of two and cannot be compared. Two measurements taken by the SAME imperfect method can be
compared, because whatever the method distorts, it distorts both times. The skill therefore
never compares its output to a published figure, and always compares it to the user's own
prior week measured the same way.

Secondary implication: roughly 60 percent of off-task time in this sample was
self-initiated. Fragmentation is not mainly something done to the user, which matters both
for which nudges are worth offering and for keeping the report's tone off blame.
