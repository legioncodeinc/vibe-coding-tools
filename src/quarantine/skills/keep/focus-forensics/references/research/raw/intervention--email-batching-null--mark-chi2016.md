# Email Duration, Batching, and Self-interruption: Patterns of Email Use on Productivity and Stress

- **Title:** Email duration, batching, and self-interruption: Patterns of email use on productivity and stress. Gloria Mark, Shamsi Iqbal, Mary Czerwinski, Paul Johns, Akane Sano. Proceedings of ACM CHI 2016.
- **URL:** https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/Home_page/Publications_files/CHI%2016%20Email%20Duration.pdf
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic (peer-reviewed conference paper)

## Why this source matters for focus-forensics

It is the archive's counterweight. The most commonly prescribed fragmentation remedy,
batching email into a few blocks per day, was tested against logged behavior and did not
produce the promised stress benefit. Any skill that ends every report with a behavioral
nudge needs this paper in front of it, because it is the evidence that a plausible,
popular, widely recommended nudge can simply fail to do what it is sold as doing.

## Method, quoted

- "We conducted an in situ study with 40 participants (20 females, 20 males)" in "a
  research division of a large corporation", each for approximately 12 workdays.
- "Computer activity at work was logged during all business hours" using "custom-built
  Windows Activity Logging software" that "tracks every open application, which window is
  in the foreground, and whether the user is interacting with that window."
- Email duration definition: "the number of seconds that the email client was in the
  foreground window, ending when the user either changed windows or the computer had no
  keyboard or mouse activity for a period of five minutes."
- Batching classification: "participants whose 3 highest hours of email use comprised 50%
  or more of their total email use" were classified as Batchers, with the remainder coded
  Consistents or Mixed strategy.

## Findings, quoted

- **The null result:** "Despite widespread claims, we found no evidence that batching email
  leads to lower stress."
- "The longer one spends on email that hour, the higher is one's stress for that hour."
- "The more time spent on email for that day, the lower the assessed productivity for that
  day."
- Self-interruption: "Participants who primarily checked email of their own volition
  reported higher productivity at days' end with higher email duration, compared to those
  who primarily check email through notifications."

## Note on how the instrument defines its terms

Two definitional details are worth copying, because they show what a duration claim
requires. Duration was measured on the FOREGROUND window, not a visible one, and it was
terminated by five minutes of keyboard and mouse inactivity. Both of those are things this
paper's instrument could observe and Littlebird cannot. A skill without foreground state
and without input activity cannot produce the quantity this paper calls duration.

## Direct implication for the skill

1. **Never prescribe batching as a proven remedy.** The best available test of it against
   logged behavior found no stress benefit. If the user's pattern suggests batching, offer
   it as an experiment with a stated way to check it, and say the published evidence is
   mixed.
2. **Volitional checking and notification-driven checking are different behaviors with
   different outcomes.** Where the capture can distinguish "the user opened the app" from
   "something pulled the user into the app", that distinction is worth more than the raw
   count. In practice snapshots rarely distinguish them, which is itself a limit to state.
3. **One behavioral nudge per report, framed as a test, is the only defensible format.**
   The literature contains a well-known intervention that fails under measurement. That is
   an argument for offering one change with a check attached, not a list of best practices.
