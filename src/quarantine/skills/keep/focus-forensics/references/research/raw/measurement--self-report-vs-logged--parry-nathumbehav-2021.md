# A systematic review and meta-analysis of discrepancies between logged and self-reported digital media use

- **Title:** A systematic review and meta-analysis of discrepancies between logged and self-reported digital media use. Douglas A. Parry, Brittany I. Davidson, Craig J. R. Sewall, Jacob T. Fisher, Hannah Mieczkowski, Daniel S. Quintana. Nature Human Behaviour, volume 5, pages 1535 to 1547, 2021.
- **URL:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-021-01117-5 (abstract also retrieved from the University of Bath research portal record, https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-discrepancies-between-lo/)
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic (peer-reviewed meta-analysis)

## Why this source matters for focus-forensics

The skill's report is going to tell a user something about their own behavior that
disagrees with their memory of the week. This paper is the evidence that the disagreement
is expected, that it runs in both directions, and that neither the memory nor a single
instrument is authoritative. It is also the strongest available warning against the
alternative design, which would be to ask the user how fragmented their week felt and
report that back.

## Abstract, quoted in full

"There is widespread public and academic interest in understanding the uses and effects of
digital media. Scholars primarily use self-report measures of the quantity or duration of
media use as proxies for more objective measures, but the validity of these self-reports
remains unclear. Advancements in data collection techniques have produced a collection of
studies indexing both self-reported and log-based measures. To assess the alignment
between these measures, we conducted a pre-registered meta-analysis of this research.
Based on 106 effect sizes, we found that self-reported media use correlates only
moderately with logged measurements, that self-reports were rarely an accurate reflection
of logged media use and that measures of problematic media use show an even weaker
association with usage logs. These findings raise concerns about the validity of findings
relying solely on self-reported measures of media use."

The abstract as published does not state the pooled correlation coefficient, the
standardized mean difference, or the proportion of studies meeting an accuracy criterion.
Those values sit in the full text, which was not retrievable in this sweep. The claims
carried forward from this source are therefore limited to the three the abstract states
directly: 106 effect sizes; only moderate correlation; self-reports rarely accurate.

## Direct implication for the skill

Two implications, both design-level.

1. **Do not ask the user to estimate their own fragmentation and do not present their
   estimate as a measurement.** Self-report of digital media use is only moderately
   correlated with logs and is rarely accurate. The user's sense that "last week was
   terrible" is a real datum about how the week felt and is worth capturing as such, but it
   is not a second measurement of the same thing and must never be averaged with the
   snapshot evidence.
2. **The disagreement is the interesting part, and the tone rule follows from it.** When
   the report and the user's memory diverge, the correct posture is curiosity about the
   divergence rather than correction of the user. The literature does not license the
   skill to tell the user their memory is wrong; it establishes that memory and logs
   routinely differ, which is a different and much weaker claim.
