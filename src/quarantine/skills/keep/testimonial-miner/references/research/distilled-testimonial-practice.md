# Distilled: testimonial practice and its regulation

Written from a fresh read of `raw/` on 2026-08-17. Every claim ends in a bracketed pointer to
the raw file it came from. If a claim in any guide in this skill does not trace back through
this file to `raw/`, it is a defect.

**This is not legal advice.** It is a research summary assembled from public sources on a single
day. Advertising law is jurisdiction-specific and it moves. Anything with money or a lawyer on
the other side of it goes to a lawyer.

## 1. Two separate legal instruments govern the same quote

| | Endorsement Guides | Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Citation | 16 CFR Part 255 [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md] | 16 CFR Part 465 [raw/ftc--reviews-rule-text--ecfr-part465.md] |
| Legal character | Administrative interpretations of Section 5 of the FTC Act. Guidance for voluntary compliance [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md] | A trade regulation rule under 15 U.S.C. 57a [raw/ftc--reviews-rule-text--ecfr-part465.md] |
| Current version | 88 FR 48102, July 26, 2023 [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md] | 89 FR 68077, Aug. 22, 2024 [raw/ftc--reviews-rule-text--ecfr-part465.md] |
| Effective | in force as guides | **October 21, 2024** [raw/ftc--reviews-rule-qa--ftc-2024.md] |
| Direct civil penalties | none of its own | yes, for knowing violations [raw/ftc--reviews-rule-qa--ftc-2024.md] |
| Governs | honesty, substantiation, typicality, material connections | fabrication, purchased sentiment, insider reviews, suppression, fake influence metrics |

They complement each other rather than one replacing the other
[raw/ftc--reviews-rule-qa--ftc-2024.md].

**The practical split for this skill.** Part 465 is about praise that is not real. Part 255 is
about real praise presented in a way that misleads. A quote bank built from genuine captured
praise is almost entirely a Part 255 problem, with one Part 465 tripwire: the incentive question
in section 6 below.

## 2. Penalty exposure, stated precisely

Part 465 "authorizes courts to impose civil penalties for knowing violations"
[raw/ftc--reviews-rule-qa--ftc-2024.md]. The press release phrases it as the ability to "seek
civil penalties against knowing violators" [raw/ftc--reviews-rule-announcement--ftc-2024.md].

The maximum civil penalty under Section 5(m)(1)(A) of the FTC Act, the provision covering
violation of a rule respecting unfair or deceptive acts or practices, is **$53,088 per
violation**, per 16 CFR 1.98 as it read on 2026-08-17, effective for violations assessed after
January 17, 2025 [raw/ftc--civil-penalty-amount--ecfr-1-98.md].

Three qualifications that must always travel with that number:

1. It is inflation-adjusted and moves. Quote it as of a date and tell the reader to re-check
   16 CFR 1.98 [raw/ftc--civil-penalty-amount--ecfr-1-98.md].
2. It attaches to **knowing** violations. Neither the FTC sources nor the law-firm analysis in
   this archive defines the mechanics of that knowledge standard
   [raw/enforcement--reviews-rule-2026--dlapiper-2026.md]. Named gap.
3. Nothing in this archive defines what counts as one violation in a review or testimonial
   context [raw/ftc--civil-penalty-amount--ecfr-1-98.md]. Named gap. So "per violation" cannot be
   multiplied out into a total by this skill or by anyone using it.

## 3. Enforcement as of mid-2026: what the FTC has actually gone after

| Date | Matter | Conduct | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dec 22, 2025 | 10 warning letters | "creating, buying, or posting fake reviews, or giving incentives for only positive reviews" [raw/ftc--warning-letters-alert--consumer-2025.md] | letters only |
| Apr 2026 complaint, Jul 15 2026 final order | TruHeight | employees posing as real users, plus free product and discounts traded for five-star ratings | $4M judgment, $750K paid, remainder suspended for inability to pay [raw/enforcement--reviews-rule-2026--dlapiper-2026.md] |
| May 2026 | Premium Home Service, FTC with Illinois AG | fake business listings, employees and relatives posting fake five-star reviews | pending, motion to dismiss filed Jul 17 2026 [raw/enforcement--reviews-rule-2026--dlapiper-2026.md] |

The ten warning letters broke down as 6 property management companies, 3 personal injury law
firms, 1 accounting firm, and **all ten shared the same pattern: money or other incentives such
as lease discounts or gift cards, given to tenants, customers or employees in exchange for
posting positive reviews** [raw/enforcement--reviews-rule-2026--dlapiper-2026.md].

The single most useful enforcement fact in the archive: in TruHeight, Rule violations were "the
only legal basis for the monetary award" even though the Rule was not the primary focus of the
case [raw/enforcement--reviews-rule-2026--dlapiper-2026.md]. Part 465 is where the money came
from.

Also load-bearing: state attorneys general enforce in parallel under state consumer protection
law [raw/enforcement--reviews-rule-2026--dlapiper-2026.md].

**Honest read for a small business.** Every enforcement target in this archive manufactured or
purchased praise. Nothing in the archive suggests exposure for accurately quoting real praise
from a real customer with permission. The realistic risk for the user of this skill is not
prosecution. It is the incentive tripwire, the results-claim disclosure, and the permission
problem.

## 4. What Part 255 requires of every quote

| Requirement | Source text | Consequence for a mined quote |
|---|---|---|
| Honest opinion | "Endorsements must reflect the honest opinions, findings, beliefs, or experience of the endorser" [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md] | The quote must be what they actually said and meant. Rewriting breaks this. |
| No deceptive implication | an endorsement "may not convey any express or implied representation that would be deceptive if made directly by the advertiser" [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md] | You cannot launder a claim you could not make yourself through a customer's mouth. |
| Bona fide user | "the endorser must have been a bona fide user of it at the time the endorsement was given" [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md] | The speaker has to have actually been a client. Prospect enthusiasm is not a testimonial. |
| Advertiser substantiation | the advertiser "must possess and rely upon adequate substantiation ... to support express and implied claims" [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md] | If the quote asserts a result, the user needs the evidence for it. Their word is not it. |
| Advertiser liability | advertisers are liable "for misleading or unsubstantiated statements made through endorsements" [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md] | The liability is the user's, not the customer's. |

Restated in the FAQ as the essential principle: "The most important principle is that an
endorsement has to represent the accurate experience and opinion of the endorser"
[raw/ftc--endorsement-faq--what-people-are-asking.md].

## 5. Results claims: the typicality rule, which is the whole ballgame

This is the most important regulatory finding in the archive.

**The default interpretation.** "An advertisement containing an endorsement relating the
experience of one or more consumers on a central or key attribute of the product will likely be
interpreted as representing that the endorser's experience is representative of what consumers
will generally achieve with the advertised product in actual conditions of use"
[raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md].

So publishing "they tripled our revenue" implies that tripling revenue is what clients generally
get. That implication is made by publishing it, whether or not anyone intended it.

**Disclaimers do not cure it.** Research demonstrates that "Results not typical" and "These
testimonials are based on the experiences of a few people and you are not likely to have similar
results" do not adequately reduce the representative-experience message. Such disclaimers "are
unlikely to be effective" [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md].

**What is required instead.** "If the advertiser does not have substantiation that the endorser's
experience is representative of what consumers will generally achieve, the advertisement should
clearly and conspicuously disclose the generally expected performance in the depicted
circumstances" [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md]. The FAQ restates it: where an
endorser claims exceptional or above average results, the advertiser needs proof those results
are typical, otherwise the ad "must make clear to the audience what the generally expected
results" are [raw/ftc--endorsement-faq--what-people-are-asking.md].

**The three-way fork this produces for any quote containing a number or a claimed outcome:**

| Situation | What the sources require |
|---|---|
| The user has substantiation that this result is typical | Publish. Keep the substantiation. |
| The result is atypical and the user knows what typical is | Publish with a clear and conspicuous disclosure of the generally expected performance. Not a "results not typical" line. |
| The user does not know what typical is | Neither option above is available. The quote cannot be published as a results claim until the user can state expected performance. |

That third row is the one the skill must enforce, and it is why the results sub-bank exists as a
separate artifact.

**Clear and conspicuous** means "difficult to miss (i.e., easily noticeable) and easily
understandable by ordinary consumers", in the same medium as the claim, and for interactive media
"the disclosure should be unavoidable" [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md]. Part
465 adds that consumers must not have to click a hyperlink to encounter a disclosure, and that a
disclosure at the beginning of a text testimonial would qualify
[raw/ftc--reviews-rule-qa--ftc-2024.md].

## 6. Material connections, and the incentive tripwire

**The standard.** A connection "that might materially affect the weight or credibility of the
endorsement" and that "is not reasonably expected by the audience" must be disclosed clearly and
conspicuously [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md].

**How wide the category is.** Business, family or personal relationship. Monetary payment. Free
or discounted product, "regardless of whether the advertiser requires an endorsement in return".
Early access. The possibility of being paid, of winning a prize, or "of appearing on television or
in other media promotions" [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md].

That last item is easy to miss and matters here: telling someone in advance that they might be
featured is itself capable of being a material connection.

**Threshold and depth.** Disclosure is needed "when a significant minority of the audience ...
does not understand or expect the connection". It "does not require the complete details of the
connection, but it must clearly communicate the nature of the connection sufficiently for
consumers to evaluate its significance" [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md].

**The timing rule that makes mined praise easier than solicited praise.** Per FAQ Q93: you can
quote customer comments in your ads; if the customers had no reason to expect compensation before
they gave their comments, there is no need to disclose a payment made afterward for permission;
if you gave them a reason to expect a benefit before they spoke, disclose it in the ad
[raw/ftc--endorsement-faq--what-people-are-asking.md].

Praise that was already given, unprompted, before any benefit existed, is the cleanest material
in the world. That is exactly the material this skill mines.

**Where the tripwire is.** Under 465.4 it is prohibited to provide "compensation or other
incentives in exchange for ... consumer reviews expressing a particular sentiment, whether
positive or negative" [raw/ftc--reviews-rule-text--ecfr-part465.md]. The marketer guide adds:
"If you offer an incentive for a review, don't condition it, explicitly or implicitly, on the
review being positive", and where an incentive was given "the review should disclose the
incentive" [raw/ftc--soliciting-reviews--ftc-marketer-guide.md]. All ten warning letters were
about incentives tied to positive reviews [raw/enforcement--reviews-rule-2026--dlapiper-2026.md].

**Consequence for a permission-request draft:** it must not offer a benefit in the same breath as
the ask. One published template does exactly that, pairing a 10 percent discount with the request
to feature a comment, and should not be copied
[raw/permission--testimonial-consent-practice--testimonialdonut-2025.md].

**Adjacent prohibited practices the same sources name:**

- Asking for reviews only from customers expected to be positive, including by geography, is
  review gating and is called out directly [raw/ftc--soliciting-reviews--ftc-marketer-guide.md,
  raw/ftc--endorsement-faq--what-people-are-asking.md].
- Asking a customer to delete or change a negative review could mislead readers. Asking them to
  add an update is acceptable [raw/ftc--endorsement-faq--what-people-are-asking.md].
- Staff, family and friends need to disclose their connection when reviewing
  [raw/ftc--soliciting-reviews--ftc-marketer-guide.md]. Officers and managers must disclose a
  material relationship under 465.5 [raw/ftc--reviews-rule-text--ecfr-part465.md]. Listing an
  employer on a profile page is not sufficient disclosure
  [raw/ftc--endorsement-faq--what-people-are-asking.md].
- You can be held responsible for what an agency or PR firm does on your behalf
  [raw/ftc--soliciting-reviews--ftc-marketer-guide.md].

## 7. Permission: which body of law actually requires it

**Not the FTC.** Nothing in Part 255 or Part 465 requires consent before quoting someone. The
FTC's own answer to "can we quote them in our ads" is yes, with disclosure consequences
[raw/ftc--endorsement-faq--what-people-are-asking.md, raw/ftc--reviews-rule-text--ecfr-part465.md].

**State right of publicity law does.** New York Civil Rights Law section 51 gives a cause of
action to "Any person whose name, portrait, picture, likeness or voice is used within this state
for advertising purposes or for the purposes of trade without the written consent first obtained"
[raw/publicity--written-consent--ny-cvr-51.md]. Remedies: injunction, damages, and where the
defendant "knowingly used" the name or likeness unlawfully, discretionary exemplary damages
[raw/publicity--written-consent--ny-cvr-51.md].

Note the standard: consent, in writing, obtained first. A second source names California Civil
Code 3344 as a parallel provision also requiring prior written consent, and states the protection
covers ordinary customers and not only celebrities; that citation is second-hand and was not
independently verified [raw/permission--testimonial-consent-practice--testimonialdonut-2025.md].

**Named gap.** This archive contains no fifty-state survey and no source on what law applies when
the business and the customer are in different states, or in different countries. It does not
resolve which state's law governs a quote published on the open internet.

**A live conflict, recorded rather than smoothed.** One source asserts that copying a public
review into your own marketing is copyright infringement because the text belongs to the reviewer
and the platform, and recommends the platform's own embed widget instead
[raw/permission--testimonial-consent-practice--testimonialdonut-2025.md]. The FTC's FAQ discusses
quoting customers in ads with no permission prerequisite at all
[raw/ftc--endorsement-faq--what-people-are-asking.md]. Both can be right: they answer different
questions, one about deceptive advertising and one about who owns the words. The source with the
copyright claim is a vendor selling testimonial-collection software and its legal assertions are
not attributed to counsel [raw/permission--testimonial-consent-practice--testimonialdonut-2025.md].

**The reading this skill adopts, stated as a preference rather than as law:** public material is
lower risk than private material, private material is not usable without a yes, and a courtesy
ask is preferred in both cases. That is also what the vendor source itself recommends as best
practice [raw/permission--testimonial-consent-practice--testimonialdonut-2025.md].

**On privacy.** Where a testimonial includes a name, photo or other identifying information the
business is handling personal data, and for EU customers the stated requirement is to explain the
use, get a clear opt-in, and keep consent records
[raw/permission--testimonial-consent-practice--testimonialdonut-2025.md]. Second-hand and
unverified against the regulation itself.

**What a release should specify:** how the content will be used, where it will appear, the
duration of use, and the scope of commercial use
[raw/permission--testimonial-consent-practice--testimonialdonut-2025.md]. A written usage
agreement naming the channels appears again in the editing source, alongside offering the customer
approval of the final edit and archiving the raw file
[raw/editing--ethical-trimming--shareone-2026.md].

## 8. Editing: the clarity-versus-meaning line

The organizing distinction, from the only source in the archive that supplies one: **clarity
edits** are acceptable, **meaning edits** are not. Stated principle: "edit for clarity, not for
persuasion" [raw/editing--ethical-trimming--shareone-2026.md].

| Clarity edits, listed as acceptable | Meaning edits, listed as prohibited |
|---|---|
| Removing filler words such as "um", "like", "you know" | Changing the core claim or outcome |
| Tightening a rambling sentence | Upgrading an adjective, "somewhat" to "dramatically" |
| Cutting an off-topic tangent | Removing a timeframe, condition or qualifier |
| Captions matching the spoken words | Splicing separate answers to create implied causation |
| Minor reordering where meaning is unchanged | Altering numbers or results |
| | Framing a personal experience as a universal guarantee |

[raw/editing--ethical-trimming--shareone-2026.md]

**Why qualifiers are load-bearing:** "'It worked after we changed our process' is different than
'It worked'". Removing the condition lets the reader "mentally fill in the blank ... with the best
possible outcome" [raw/editing--ethical-trimming--shareone-2026.md]. That is the mechanism by
which a truthful trim becomes a false impression.

**Corroboration from the primary sources.** Part 255 requires the endorsement to reflect the
endorser's honest opinion and forbids conveying an implied representation that would be deceptive
if the advertiser made it directly [raw/ftc--endorsement-guides-text--ecfr-part255.md]. The FTC's
FAQ treats a marketer's misleading alteration of a real review as the marketer's problem and as a
reportable complaint to the FTC, a consumer protection office, or the BBB
[raw/ftc--endorsement-faq--what-people-are-asking.md]. And the FTC's own summary of the rule lists
AI-generated fake reviews among the prohibited categories
[raw/ftc--reviews-rule-announcement--ftc-2024.md], which is directly on point for a language model
asked to improve a customer's wording.

**Named gap.** No source in the archive defines an ellipsis convention, addresses bracketed
insertions, or addresses combining two quotes from the same person on different dates
[raw/editing--ethical-trimming--shareone-2026.md]. Any rule this skill states on those three
points is a house standard built on top of the clarity-versus-meaning line, and must be labeled
as a house standard, not as a legal requirement.

## 9. What makes a quote persuasive rather than generic

| Finding | Source | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Testimonials on a company's own site draw skepticism. Participants said "the website would of course include only positive reviews". External review sites carry greater credibility | [raw/persuasion--web-credibility--nngroup-2016.md] | Observed user research, but from 2016 and with no stated participant count |
| "People have learned to trust these external sources more than company-sponsored content". Every participant said they read reviews before hiring a service | [raw/persuasion--web-credibility--nngroup-2016.md] | Same caveat |
| For infrequently purchased items, an explicit recommendation is more persuasive than a repurchase intention; for frequently purchased items the reverse | [raw/persuasion--review-persuasiveness--hbr-2025.md] | 111,728 reviews plus experiments with 2,000+ participants, but studied on retail product reviews |
| The brochure test: "Cut any sentence that could appear in a product brochure" | [raw/format--b2b-case-study--tatarek-2026.md] | Practitioner assertion, no study |
| Concrete imagery beats abstraction. "I was literally running around the building with manila folders, chasing down receipts" versus "significant improvement" | [raw/format--b2b-case-study--tatarek-2026.md] | Practitioner assertion |
| A before-and-after phrasing beats a percentage: "nearly a week to under five hours" beats "90% faster" | [raw/format--b2b-case-study--tatarek-2026.md] | Practitioner assertion |
| Numbers are not required. A case study with no metrics can work on a line like "This is literally changing my life" | [raw/format--b2b-case-study--tatarek-2026.md] | Practitioner assertion, with a named example |
| Specific context builds credibility even without a name: "A 75-person brand experience agency in the UK carries more weight than a mid-sized agency" | [raw/format--b2b-case-study--tatarek-2026.md] | Practitioner assertion |

**Inference, labeled as one.** Professional services are infrequent purchases, so on the HBR
finding an explicit recommendation should outrank a bare satisfaction statement in a services
quote bank. Extending a retail-product-review study to B2B services quotes is an inference and not
a finding [raw/persuasion--review-persuasiveness--hbr-2025.md].

**Two findings that pull in the same direction and together justify the permission tier as a
product feature, not just a compliance chore.** Users trust external, checkable sources over
company-sponsored content [raw/persuasion--web-credibility--nngroup-2016.md], and a public review
or public post is exactly such a source, while a DM exists only inside the company's own
marketing. Over-polishing a wall of praise on an owned page pushes it further into the category
users reported trusting least [raw/persuasion--web-credibility--nngroup-2016.md].

## 10. Format guidance for where quotes end up

Eight-section case study structure: title naming the customer and headlining the result;
narrative introduction; two or three sentence TLDR; before and after table; challenge in the
customer's own language; solution; results leading with the primary metric then quotes; future
plus a single call to action [raw/format--b2b-case-study--tatarek-2026.md].

Craft rules from the same source: use the customer's actual words at a rate of roughly one quote
every two or three paragraphs; let the customer be the protagonist; set expectations upfront on
timeline, stakeholders and approval; send the draft to the customer champion before their legal
or PR team sees it. Do not write like a press release; do not tour features instead of outcomes;
do not try to cover every persona in one study; **do not invent percentages and do not skip the
results section when metrics are unavailable** [raw/format--b2b-case-study--tatarek-2026.md].

Broader credibility context: users judge a site on design quality, upfront disclosure of prices
and policies, comprehensive and current content, and whether the business is connected to the rest
of the web through social media, review sites and news outlets. An isolated site "appear[s] to
either have something to hide or not be a fully established, stable company"
[raw/persuasion--web-credibility--nngroup-2016.md].

## 11. Conflicts recorded, not resolved

1. **Does quoting a public review require permission?** Vendor source says yes on copyright
   grounds [raw/permission--testimonial-consent-practice--testimonialdonut-2025.md]. FTC discusses
   quoting customers with no permission prerequisite
   [raw/ftc--endorsement-faq--what-people-are-asking.md]. Different bodies of law, different
   questions. Preferred reading: both apply, the FTC governs deception and state and IP law govern
   ownership and identity, so a courtesy ask is the cheap way to satisfy all of them at once.
2. **Is a percentage or a before-and-after phrasing better?** One practitioner source prefers
   before-and-after [raw/format--b2b-case-study--tatarek-2026.md]. Nothing else in the archive
   addresses it. Recorded as a single unreplicated preference, not a rule.
3. **How much do on-site testimonials actually convert?** The 2026 marketing sources found in the
   sweep assert conversion lift with no method behind it, so none were archived. The only observed
   evidence retrieved says users discount on-site testimonials
   [raw/persuasion--web-credibility--nngroup-2016.md]. That evidence is a decade old. This
   archive cannot say what a testimonial is worth, only what makes one credible.
