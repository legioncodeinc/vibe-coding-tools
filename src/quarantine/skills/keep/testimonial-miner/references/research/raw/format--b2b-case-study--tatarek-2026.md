# How to Write a B2B SaaS Case Study That Buyers Actually Trust

- **Author:** Pawel Tatarek
- **Published:** February 27, 2026. Updated April 9, 2026.

- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** practitioner blog. No study behind it. Archived for its craft taxonomy, which

## The eight-section structure it recommends

1. Title, naming the customer and headlining the result
2. Introduction, a narrative hook
3. Overview or TLDR, two or three sentences covering customer, challenge and outcome
4. Before and after table
5. Challenge, described in the customer's own language
6. Solution, broken into subsections where there are multiple use cases
7. Results, leading with the primary metric, then secondary results and customer quotes
8. Future and a single clear call to action

## Credible quote versus generic quote

Stated test, quoted: "Cut any sentence that could appear in a product brochure."

The example of a credible quote given: a finance specialist saying "I was literally running
around the building with manila folders, chasing down receipts."

Generic claims such as "significant improvement" are called out as lacking the concrete imagery
that persuades a skeptical buyer.

That brochure test is directly implementable as a screening rule. A quote that could be a
sentence of your own marketing copy is a weak quote regardless of how warm it is.

## Numbers

Advice is to leave a customer interview with "at least one quotable number", and where an exact
figure is unavailable, to bracket it in conversation: "Would you say it cut the time in half, or
more like 20 to 30%?"

Preference stated for concrete before-and-after phrasing over a percentage: "nearly a week to
under five hours" beats "90% faster."

Counter-example the source supplies itself: a Dropbox case study by Writer contains no specific
numbers and relies on a designer's line, "This is literally changing my life."

So numbers are not mandatory for a quote to work. That matters for this skill, because the
numbered quotes are precisely the ones that carry the heaviest legal load.

## Identifying the speaker

Include role, company context, industry and scale. On anonymized cases, quoted: "A 75-person
brand experience agency in the UK carries more weight than a mid-sized agency." Specificity
builds credibility even without a name.

This gives the skill a usable fallback for the confidential tier: a quote that cannot be
attributed by name may still be usable in a de-identified but specific form, if and only if the
speaker agrees.

## Dos, as listed

- Use the customer's actual words. Aim for a quote every two or three paragraphs
- Let the customer be the protagonist, the product is the tool
- Set expectations upfront on timeline, stakeholders and the approval process
- Send drafts to the customer champion before their legal or PR team sees it

## Don'ts, as listed

- Do not write like a press release, heavy on superlatives and light on specifics
- Do not tour features instead of showing outcomes
- Do not try to cover every persona in one case study
- **Do not invent percentages, and do not skip the results section when metrics are unavailable**

That last one is the only place in the non-legal half of this archive where fabrication is named
directly, and it names the exact failure mode a language model is most likely to commit when
asked to produce a results quote from thin material.
