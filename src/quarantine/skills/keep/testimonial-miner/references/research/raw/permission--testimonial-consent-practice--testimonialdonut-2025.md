# Do I Need Permission to Use Customer Testimonials?

- **Title:** Do I Need Permission to Use Customer Testimonials? A 2025 Guide (Testimonial Donut)
- **URL:** https://testimonialdonut.com/resources/do-i-need-permission-to-use-customer-testimonials
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Published:** July 4, 2025
- **Source type:** vendor-blog (testimonial-collection software company, so it has a commercial
  interest in the answer being "yes, use our tool"). Legal assertions on the page are not
  attributed to counsel and several are stated more confidently than the underlying law supports.
  Treated here as practice guidance, not as legal authority. Where it cites a statute, the
  statute is the citable thing.

## The headline position

"You must ask for permission to use a testimonial from the customer before using their name or
likeness in a testimonial."

## Public reviews versus private communications

**Public platform reviews (Google, Yelp, Facebook):**

- The page asserts that copying and pasting a review into your own marketing violates copyright,
  on the theory that the text belongs to the reviewer and the platform.
- Recommended alternative: use the platform's official embed or widget.
- Recommended regardless: ask permission before reusing in a new context.

**Private messages and emails:**

- Treat direct communications from a customer as owned by the customer.
- A written consent given by email reply counts as valid permission.
- Document all consent.

**Conflict to record, not smooth over.** This source says copying a public review into marketing
is copyright infringement. The FTC's own FAQ (Q93,
`ftc--endorsement-faq--what-people-are-asking.md`) says a business may quote customer comments in
its ads and discusses only the disclosure consequences, never a permission prerequisite. These are
different bodies of law answering different questions: the FTC is answering "is this deceptive
advertising," and this vendor is answering "do you own the words." Both can be true at once.
The practical resolution adopted by this skill is to treat public material as lower risk than
private material while still preferring a courtesy ask, which is what this source itself
recommends as best practice.

## What a release should cover

For video and photo testimonials, a signed release specifying:

- How the content will be used (website, social media, ads)
- Where it will appear
- Duration of the usage
- Scope of commercial use

"Make sure they understand they're granting usage rights."

## Right of publicity

The page states that individuals have protected rights in their identity under US law, and names
"California's Civil Code 3344", which it describes as making it unlawful to use someone's name,
voice, photo, or likeness for advertising without prior written consent. It states this applies
to ordinary customers, not only celebrities.

That is a second state statute pointing the same direction as
`publicity--written-consent--ny-cvr-51.md`. The California citation was not independently
verified against the statute in this sweep. Recorded as a second-hand citation.

## Privacy

On GDPR: where a testimonial includes names, photos, or identifiable information, the business is
handling personal data. Stated requirement: "Always tell EU customers how you'll use their data
and get a clear opt-in. Keep consent records."

On FTC: testimonials must reflect genuine opinions, and compensation must be disclosed.

## Permission-request wording the page supplies

Basic email format:

> "Hi [name], thank you for your feedback. Would it be okay for us to feature your comment on our
> website? Just reply 'YES' if you're comfortable."

With an incentive:

> "We'd love to offer you a 10% discount on your next booking. Would it be okay for us to feature
> your comment on our website?"

With social proof framing:

> "Many clients have shared their experiences publicly. We'd be honored to add your feedback. May
> we share your comment on our testimonials page?"

Suggested follow-up sequence: day 1 initial request, day 3 polite reminder, day 7 final mention
with urgency framing.

**Two of those three templates should not be copied.** The incentive template offers a discount
in the same breath as the request to feature a comment, which is exactly the pattern the FTC's
warning letters targeted (`enforcement--reviews-rule-2026--dlapiper-2026.md`), even though here
the praise already exists and is not being purchased. The social-proof template applies peer
pressure to a consent decision. The basic template is clean and is the one worth adapting.

The day-7 "urgency framing" step is also inappropriate for a consent request. Urgency is a
conversion tactic; consent is not a conversion.

## Concluding line from the source

"Ask upfront, document consent, and honour any privacy concerns."
