# The Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule: Questions and Answers

- **Title:** The Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule: Questions and Answers (FTC business
  guidance)
- **URL:** https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** official-docs (FTC staff business guidance)

## The effective date

**The rule became effective October 21, 2024.** This is the authoritative statement of the
effective date in this archive. The Federal Register publication date, 2024-08-22, appears in
the codified source note and is a different thing.

## Scope and who is covered

The rule applies to businesses broadly. Ordinary consumers cannot be held liable for the content
of their own reviews. Liability can reach "influencers who are in the business of posting
testimonials," advertising agencies, PR firms, and review brokers.

A retailer merely hosting user-submitted reviews is exempt under 465.2 unless it "wrote or bought
them." **That hosting exemption disappears the moment a review is featured in advertising
material: featured reviews become testimonials and lose the hosting exemption.** This is the
sentence that matters most for a skill that lifts reviews into marketing.

## Civil penalties

The rule "authorizes courts to impose civil penalties for knowing violations." This page does
not state a dollar figure. The figure lives in
`ftc--civil-penalty-amount--ecfr-1-98.md`.

Note the qualifier "knowing." Civil penalty exposure attaches to knowing violations, not to every
technical violation.

## Clear and conspicuous

Disclosures must be unavoidable: consumers cannot be required to click a hyperlink to encounter
them. The page states a disclosure at the beginning of a text testimonial would qualify. A
hashtag such as "#Ad" might work in context but may be too easy to miss in video.

## Interaction with the Endorsement Guides

The rule complements the Endorsement Guides at 16 CFR Part 255 rather than replacing them. The
page notes that "failing to disclose incentives could be a violation of the FTC Act."

Two separate legal instruments therefore apply to the same quote: the Guides (interpretive, no
civil penalties directly, backed by Section 5) and the Rule (civil penalties for knowing
violations).

## Directly relevant answers

**Soliciting reviews.** Businesses can solicit reviews from purchasers generally without
liability, including where an employee responds to the solicitation.

**Featuring reviews in ads.** Reviews featured in advertising materials become testimonials and
lose the hosting exemption.

**Actors.** Using actors to portray testimonialists is not covered by the Rule itself but "could
be deceptive in violation of the FTC Act."

**Incentivized reviews.** Offering an incentive for a review is permitted if there is no express
or implied requirement that the review be positive. Paying consumers to remove negative reviews
"may violate the FTC Act as an unfair or deceptive act."

**Selective use of positive reviews.** Organizing reviews by helpfulness or by default rating
order is permitted. Businesses can exclude irrelevant or off-topic reviews if they do so
consistently. **The rule does not cover selectively using only positive reviews in marketing,
though the page notes such practices could violate the FTC Act separately.** So cherry-picking
which of your real quotes to publish is not a Part 465 violation. It is not a blanket permission
either.
