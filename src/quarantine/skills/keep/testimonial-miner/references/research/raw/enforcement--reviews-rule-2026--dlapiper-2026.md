# FTC's 2026 enforcement approach to fake reviews takes shape

- **Title:** FTC's 2026 enforcement approach to fake reviews takes shape: Takeaways for companies
  (DLA Piper insight)
- **URL:** https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/07/ftcs-2026-enforcement-approach-to-fake-reviews-takes-shape-takeaways-for-companies
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** law-firm analysis (secondary, but the only source found that maps enforcement
  activity under Part 465 as of mid-2026). Case facts below are as reported by this source and
  were not independently verified against court dockets.

## Status of the rule as of mid-2026

In effect since late 2024. The FTC has moved to targeted enforcement through both direct actions
and warning-letter campaigns.

## Named actions

**Premium Home Service, May 2026**

- Parties: Premium Home Service and its owner. Brought by the FTC together with the Illinois
  Attorney General.
- Forum: US District Court for the Northern District of Illinois.
- Allegations: creating fake business listings, directing employees and relatives to post fake
  five-star reviews, and posting fake reviews on Apple's App Store.
- Status as reported: pending. Defendants filed a motion to dismiss on July 17, 2026.
- Penalties: not determined, case ongoing.
- Quoted from Commissioner Mark Meador's concurring statement: "A particularly troubling aspect
  of this case is how the fraud as alleged defeated the efforts of diligent consumers".

**TruHeight, complaint April 2026, settlement July 2026**

- Parties: TruHeight, a supplement company, and two principals.
- Allegations: reviews posted by employees posing as real users, and reviews from consumers
  offered free product or discounts in exchange for five-star ratings.
- Outcome: $4 million judgment, $750,000 due immediately, the remainder suspended for inability
  to pay. Final order July 15, 2026.
- Reported detail that matters: Rule violations were "the only legal basis for the monetary
  award" even though the Rule was not the case's primary focus.

That last point is the strongest available evidence that Part 465 is not decorative. It supplied
the money in a case that was about something else.

## The warning-letter campaign, unpacked

The December 2025 announcement disclosed 10 letters without naming recipients. Between April and
July 2026 the FTC posted copies following FOIA requests, revealing recipients and allegations.

Reported breakdown of the ten:

| Sector | Letters |
|---|---|
| Property management companies | 6 |
| Personal injury law firms | 3 |
| Accounting firms | 1 |

Common pattern across all ten: providing "money or other incentives (e.g., lease discounts or
gift cards) to tenants, customers, or employees, in exchange for posting ... positive reviews."

The source notes the FTC offered no explanation for targeting these industries, and observes an
apparent focus on "consequential" services such as housing, legal representation and financial
advice.

## Penalty standard

The source states that "knowing violations of the Rule may subject companies to civil penalties
and possible consumer restitution" but does not elaborate on the mechanics of the knowledge
standard. That leaves the meaning of "knowing" undefined in this archive. Named gap.

## Compliance takeaways as stated

- Companies should "assess their review campaigns as well as how they design and moderate review
  content on websites or other platforms."
- Businesses in more consequential service categories face heightened scrutiny.
- State attorneys general also enforce under state consumer protection law, as in the Premium
  Home Service case.
- Rule violations often emerge as secondary allegations inside broader deceptive-conduct
  investigations.
- "This is no time to be complacent about compliance when it comes to consumer reviews."

## Reading for a solo operator or small business

Every enforcement target in this source manufactured or bought praise. Nothing here suggests
exposure for a business that quotes real praise from real customers accurately and with
permission. The pattern of the ten letters is worth restating plainly: incentives tied to
sentiment, in all ten cases. That is the behavior a testimonial-gathering workflow can drift into
without meaning to.
