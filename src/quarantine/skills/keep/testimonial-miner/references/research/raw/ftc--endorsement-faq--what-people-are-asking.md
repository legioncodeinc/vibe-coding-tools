# The FTC's Endorsement Guides: What People Are Asking

- **Title:** The FTC's Endorsement Guides: What People Are Asking (FTC business guidance FAQ)

## The question that governs quoting a customer (Q93, verbatim question text)

"My company wants to contact customers and interview them about their experiences with our
service. If we like what they say about our service, can we ask them to allow us to quote them
in our ads? Can we pay them for letting us use their endorsements?"

Answer, paraphrased from the FTC's text: you can quote customer comments in your ads. If the
customers had no reason to expect compensation before they gave their comments, there is no need
to disclose payments made afterward for permission to use them. If you gave the customers a
reason to expect a benefit before they spoke, disclose that fact in the ad.

**This is the load-bearing finding for the permission tier design.** The FTC's own position is
that quoting a customer in an ad is permissible, and that the timing of any benefit relative to
the praise is what drives the disclosure obligation. Praise given first, permission bought
second, is treated differently from a benefit dangled before the praise.

## The honest-experience principle (Q83, verbatim question text)

"Besides disclosing my relationship with the company whose product I'm endorsing, what are the
essential things I need to know about endorsements?"

Answer, verbatim excerpt: "The most important principle is that an endorsement has to represent
the accurate experience and opinion of the endorser: You can't talk about your experience with a
product if you haven't tried it ... You can't otherwise misrepresent your experience with or
opinions about a product."

## Altered reviews (Q42, verbatim question text)

"I posted a review of a service on a website. Now the marketer has taken my review and changed it
in a way I think is misleading. Am I liable for that? What can I do?"

Answer, paraphrased: the reviewer is not liable for changes the marketer made. The reviewer
should contact the marketer, and may complain to the FTC, a local consumer protection office, or
the Better Business Bureau.

**Read the direction of that answer carefully.** The FTC treats a marketer's misleading
alteration of a real review as the marketer's problem and as a reportable complaint. It is the
clearest statement in the archive that editing a quote into something better is a live
enforcement-adjacent risk, not a style question.

## Review gating (Q89, verbatim question text)

"My company would like to get more online reviews by sending a link to a review page to customers
in specific regions where we have the highest customer satisfaction. We would not send a link to
customers in areas where we know our customer experience is poor. Is that OK?"

Answer, paraphrased: no. Soliciting only from segments expected to be satisfied biases the
resulting body of reviews and is misleading, even without an explicit request for positive
reviews.

## Asking unhappy customers to change a review (Q91, verbatim question text)

"Is there anything wrong with contacting customers who left negative reviews and trying to make
them happy? If I succeed, can I ask them to change their reviews?"

Answer, paraphrased: contacting unhappy customers and asking them to add an update is
acceptable. Asking them to delete or change the original negative review could mislead readers.

## Discounts for saying good things (Q94, verbatim question text)

"I'm starting a new online business and I don't have money for advertising, so I need publicity.
Can I tell people that if they say good things about my business on a third-party review
platform, I'll give them a discount on items they buy through my website?"

Answer, paraphrased: no, you cannot condition an incentive on the review being positive. You may
invite all customers to post honest reviews and provide compensation, provided the compensation
is disclosed in the review.

## Employee and insider endorsements (Q117 and Q118, verbatim question text plus quoted answers)

Q117: "I work for a terrific company. Can I mention our products to people in my social networks?
My 'friends' and followers won't be misled since it's clear in my online profiles where I work.
How about on a review site?"

Answer excerpt, verbatim: "You should disclose your relationship to the company ... listing your
employer on your profile page isn't enough. After all, people who just read what you post won't
get that information."

Q118: "On their own initiative and without us asking, several of our employees used their
personal social network accounts to 'share' one of our company's posts. Do they need to disclose
that they work for our company?"

Answer excerpt, verbatim: "Whether there should be any disclosure depends upon whether the
'shares' could be viewed as promoting one of your company's products or services. If so,
employees endorsing the post should disclose their relationship to the company."

Q119 answer excerpt, verbatim: "It wouldn't be reasonable to expect you to monitor every social
media posting by all of your employees. However, you should establish a formal program to remind
employees periodically of your policy."

## Typicality, as restated in the FAQ

Where an endorser claims exceptional or even above average results, the advertiser must have
proof those results are typical, otherwise "an ad featuring that endorser must make clear to the
audience what the generally expected results" are.

## What the FAQ does NOT answer

Scanned the full 119-question list. There is **no question on the page about whether a business
may edit or shorten a customer testimonial for length**, and **no question about using a
customer's private email or direct message as a testimonial**. Both are named gaps in this
archive. The nearest coverage is Q42 above, which addresses alteration only from the reviewer's
side.
