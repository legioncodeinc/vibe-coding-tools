# Ironies of Automation

- **URL:** https://ckrybus.com/static/papers/Bainbridge_1983_Automatica.pdf
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic (Lisanne Bainbridge, Automatica, volume 19 number 6, 1983,
  pages 775 to 779)
- **Why archived:** The foundational statement of what happens to a job when the routine
  part of it is automated. It is 43 years old and it is the single most load-bearing source
  in this archive, because it is the argument for why "this task repeats, therefore automate
  it" is an incomplete inference.

## The residual task irony, quoted

> "the designer who tries to eliminate the operator still leaves the operator to do the
> tasks which the designer cannot think how to automate"

The consequence stated in the paper: the operator is left with an arbitrary collection of
tasks, and little thought may have been given to providing support for them.

This is the direct hit on naive automation candidate selection. Automating the mechanical
portion of a task does not leave a smaller version of the same task. It leaves the residue,
which is by construction the part that resisted automation, and which now has to be done
without the run-up the mechanical part used to provide.

## The designer error irony, quoted

> "designer errors can be a major source of operating problems"

The automation is only as good as the model its author had of the task. Where that model is
wrong, the error is now systematic and repeated rather than occasional.

## Manual skill decay, quoted

> "physical skills deteriorate when they are not used, particularly the refinements of gain
> and timing."

And the consequence:

> "a formerly experienced operator who has been monitoring an automated process may now be
> an inexperienced one."

## The monitoring paradox, quoted

> "the automatic control system has been put in because it can do the job better than the
> operator, but yet the operator is being asked to monitor that it is working effectively."

Bainbridge's judgment on this: the human monitor has been given an impossible task when
required to verify decisions a computer makes better than a human can.

## The skill requirement inversion, quoted

> "one can argue that the operator needs to be more rather than less skilled, and less
> rather than more loaded, than average"

## Scope caveat

The paper is about industrial process control: continuous plants, control rooms, manual
takeover under fault conditions. The mechanisms transfer to knowledge work by analogy, not
by measurement. The transfer is strongest for the residual task irony and the monitoring
paradox, and weakest for physical skill decay, which is literally about hand-eye control
loops.
