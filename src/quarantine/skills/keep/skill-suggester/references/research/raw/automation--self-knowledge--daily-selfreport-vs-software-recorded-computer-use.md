# Daily self-reports resulted in information bias when assessing exposure duration to computer use

- **URL:** https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/192129/cdc_192129_DS1.pdf
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic (occupational epidemiology study, distributed via CDC Stacks)
- **Why archived:** A same-day comparison of self-reported computer use against
  software-recorded use. Same-day is the important property: it removes the long recall
  window that a critic could blame Collopy 1996 on, and the bias survives anyway.

## Design and sample

- Repeated-measures observational design across three weeks of an academic semester, sampled
  at the beginning, middle and end.
- 30 undergraduate students, 15 male and 15 female, mean age 20.7 with standard deviation
  1.5, from a single dormitory.
- Self-reports collected on handheld computers roughly four times daily. 2,132 reports
  collected in total.
- Usage-monitoring software on the personal computers recorded keyboard and mouse activity
  as the objective reference.

## The magnitude of the bias

- Median self-reported duration exceeded software-recorded duration by 1.9 hours.
- Quoted: "self-reports generally overestimated computer use when software-recorded
  durations were less than 3.6 hr, and underestimated when above 3.6 hr."
- The fitted relationship: Difference = 2.26 hr minus 0.63 times software-recorded duration.

That regression-to-the-middle pattern is the same one Collopy 1996 reports on a completely
different population two decades earlier: light use overestimated, heavy use underestimated.

## Correlations

- Spearman correlations within participants ranged 0.22 to 0.8, median 0.53.
- Cross-sectional comparison using median values per participant: 0.33.

## A second-order finding worth keeping

Quoted: "Experiencing symptoms was related to a 0.15-hr increase in self-reported duration
after controlling for software-recorded duration."

How the person felt changed their reported duration independently of the actual duration.
Applied to automation candidate selection: the tasks a person nominates as their repetitive
burden are partly selected by how annoying the task is, not by how often or how long it
actually runs.

## Authors' conclusion

Daily self-reported computer-use duration had a weak-to-moderate correlation with
software-recorded duration, producing both non-differential and differential information
bias that could distort findings.

## Caveat

Small sample, undergraduate population, single dormitory. It is corroboration of a
direction, not a precise effect size for working professionals.
