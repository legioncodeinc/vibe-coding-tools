# Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation: An Attentional Integration

- **URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720810376055
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic (Parasuraman and Manzey, Human Factors, volume 52 number 3,
  2010)
- **Why archived:** The measured follow-up to Bainbridge. It supplies the mechanism by which
  a person stops checking an automation's output, and the conditions under which that
  happens. It is the evidence behind the rule that a proposed automation whose output the
  user must verify anyway is a bad proposal.

## Stated objective, quoted

> "Objective: Our aim was to review empirical studies of complacency and bias in human
> interaction with automated and decision support systems and provide an integrated
> theoretical model for their explanation."

## Automation complacency

Defined as reduced monitoring vigilance, and the condition is specific, quoted:

> "occurs under conditions of multiple-task load, when manual tasks compete with the
> automated task for the operator's attention."

The condition matters. Complacency is not a claim that people are lazy about automation in
general. It is a claim about attention under competing load, which is the ordinary state of
a person running several tools at once.

## Automation bias

Acceptance of automated suggestions regardless of their accuracy, producing, quoted:

> "both omission and commission errors when decision aids are imperfect."

- **Omission error.** The automation did not flag something, and the person did not act.
- **Commission error.** The automation suggested something wrong, and the person acted on
  it.

Omission is the harder one to catch, because nothing appears in the output to inspect.

## The integrated conclusion, quoted

> "complacency and automation bias represent different manifestations of overlapping
> automation-induced phenomena, with attention playing a central role."

The review also reports that both phenomena occur across expertise levels and resist simple
training interventions. That last point is the one that turns this into a design rule
instead of a training note: telling the user to stay vigilant does not work, so the
proposal has to be shaped so vigilance is not the control.
