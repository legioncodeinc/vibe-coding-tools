# Morning Routines and Mental Health: What Science Actually Shows

- **URL:** https://www.simplypsychology.com/articles/morning-routine-mental-health
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** community (Simply Psychology, secondary review written for a general
  audience)

## Practices the article says are supported

- **Sleep timing consistency.** "consistent sleep timing (going to bed and waking at the
  same time each day) supports better mood, cognitive function, and overall mental health
  outcomes."
- **Exercise.** Described as having "among the strongest evidence bases in mental health
  research", with timing less important than consistency.
- **Morning daylight.** "Morning light signals the suprachiasmatic nucleus (the brain's
  master clock) to calibrate the day's rhythm" and is "one of the better-supported morning
  habits".
- **Cortisol awakening response.** A rise in the first 30 to 45 minutes after waking is
  "normal and healthy"; blunted responses are linked to burnout and depression.
- **Decision reduction.** Consistent routines reduce decision fatigue, described as a
  "real psychological phenomenon".

## Claims the article says are NOT supported

- **Waking early.** "No strong research establishes that waking early is independently
  beneficial for mental health across all chronotypes."
- **Longer and more elaborate routines.** Research "does not suggest that longer or more
  complex is better. A 15-minute consistent routine may provide as much structure benefit
  as a 90-minute elaborate sequence."
- **Morning journaling as practised in routine culture.** Expressive writing has evidence,
  but "the journaling usually studied is directed, emotionally engaged writing, not the
  gratitude lists and intention-setting that morning routine culture tends to emphasize."

## Source quality note

No individual studies are cited with numbers in this piece. It is archived as a
secondary review that is unusually explicit about which popular claims lack support, which
is the specific thing that is hard to find in this literature. Any claim taken from it is
reported as a secondary summary, never as a primary result.
