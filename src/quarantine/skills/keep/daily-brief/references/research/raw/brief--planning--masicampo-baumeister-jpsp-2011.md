# Consider It Done! Plan Making Can Eliminate the Cognitive Effects of Unfulfilled Goals

- **URL:** https://users.wfu.edu/masicaej/MasicampoBaumeister2011JPSP.pdf
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic (Masicampo and Baumeister, Journal of Personality and Social
  Psychology, 2011)

## Hypothesis

"The core hypothesis for the present work was therefore that much of the cognitive
activity associated with goals may cease once a plan is made, even though the goal remains
unfulfilled."

## Design

Six studies.

## What unfulfilled goals cost

Three measured decrements:

- "Unfinished goals caused intrusive thoughts during an unrelated reading task (Studies 1
  and 5B)"
- "high mental accessibility of goal-related words (Studies 2 and 3)"
- "poor performance on an unrelated anagram task (Study 4)"

## What plan making did

"Allowing participants to formulate specific plans for their unfulfilled goals eliminated
the various activation and interference effects."

Note the mechanism precisely: the goal was still unfulfilled. Only the plan changed.

## Statistics as reported

| Study | N | Measure | Statistic |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 69 | Intrusive thoughts | F(2, 68) = 4.67, p = .013 |
| 2 | 68 | Goal accessibility | F(2, 67) = 6.58, p = .01 |
| 3 | 124 | Means accessibility | F(2, 124) = 3.07, p = .05 |
| 4 | 97 | Anagrams solved | F(2, 96) = 3.34, p = .040 |

## Conclusion, quoted

"Committing to a specific plan for a goal may therefore not only facilitate attainment of
the goal but may also free cognitive resources for other pursuits."

## Caveats to carry forward

Laboratory studies with student samples, small Ns, published in 2011 and therefore before
the field's replication reforms. The effect direction is consistent across six studies but
the individual p values sit close to threshold. Use this to justify the *shape* of a
recommendation (name a specific plan, not a bare task) rather than to promise an effect
size.
