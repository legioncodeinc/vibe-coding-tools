# The Irrational Hungry Judge Effect Revisited: Simulations Reveal That the Magnitude of the Effect Is Overestimated

- **Also indexed at:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/irrational-hungry-judge-effect-revisited-simulations-reveal-that-the-magnitude-of-the-effect-is-overestimated/61CE825D4DC137675BB9CAD04571AE58
- **Fetched:** 2026-08-17
- **Source type:** academic (Glockner, Judgment and Decision Making, 2016)

## The original claim

Danziger et al. (2011) reported that favorable parole decisions fell from roughly 65% at
the start of a session to near 0% by its end, recovering to about 65% after meal breaks,
and attributed the pattern to mental depletion.

## Criticism one, from Weinshall-Margel and Shapard

Case ordering is not random. Unrepresented prisoners tend to appear later in sessions and
receive fewer favorable rulings. The ordering effect is confounded with case composition.

## Criticism two, Glockner's simulation

Favorable rulings take longer than unfavorable ones: 7.37 minutes versus 5.21 minutes.
A judge who rationally avoids starting a long case shortly before a break produces a
session whose final cases are disproportionately the short ones, which are the unfavorable
ones. The simulations show that rational time management alone can produce drops of 15% to
45% with no depletion whatsoever.

## Conclusions, quoted

"the effect of serial order and mental depletion is overestimated in the original work."

"large parts, but admittedly not all aspects" of the original findings reflect statistical
artifacts rather than genuine decision fatigue.

The real effect, if present, is substantially smaller than reported and potentially
negligible relative to laboratory measures of mental depletion.

## How to use this

This is the counterweight. The most-cited evidence for "decision quality degrades across a
session" is largely an artifact. Any design that leans on decision fatigue must state that
the flagship result is contested and must not claim a large effect. See the Linder 2014
source in this archive for a measured effect that survives with a much smaller magnitude.
