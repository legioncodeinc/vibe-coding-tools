## Working distance for a head-and-shoulders / headshot framing (full-frame equivalents)
| Focal length | Headshot working distance |
|---|---|
| **35 mm** | **3–5 ft (0.9–1.5 m)** |
| **50 mm** | **4–6 ft (1.2–1.8 m)** |
| **85 mm** | **6–10 ft (1.8–3.0 m)** — the second source narrows it to **6–8 ft** |
| **135 mm** | **10–15 ft (3.0–4.6 m)** — second source says **12–15 ft or more** |
| **200 mm** | **20+ ft (6+ m)** |

Extrapolating the same framing downward (not in these sources but implied by the linear relationship): **24 mm** headshot distance ≈ **2–3 ft (0.6–0.9 m)** — i.e. the phone-selfie geometry.

## Why 85–135 mm is the classic portrait range
- **85 mm** gives "just enough compression to flatten out any weird distortions" while keeping the working distance conversational; background is "beautifully out of focus, but you will still be able to tell" where you are. Typical apertures **f/1.4–f/1.8**.
- **135 mm** "pulls the background dramatically closer to your subject"; background "melts into an absolute wash of color," "a creamy, abstract painting." Typical apertures **f/2.8–f/3.5** (already enough separation at that distance).
- The trade: 85 mm keeps environmental context and lets you talk to the subject; 135 mm erases context and forces a shout across the room.

## Underlying rule
Perspective is set by **distance**, not focal length. Focal length only determines what distance you must stand at for a given framing. So:
- Short lens + close = nose/forehead enlarged, ears shrink, face reads rounded and "amateur/intimate."
- Long lens + far = facial planes flatten, nose and ears normalize, face reads "professional/aloof."
