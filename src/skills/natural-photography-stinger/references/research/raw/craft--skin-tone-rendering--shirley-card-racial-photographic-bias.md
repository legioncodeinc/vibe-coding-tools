# The Shirley Card: Racial Photographic Bias through Skin Tone (Shutterstock Blog)
- URL: https://www.shutterstock.com/blog/shirley-card-racial-photographic-bias
- Fetched: 2026-08-17
- Source type: education
- Note: historical/foundational context for why imaging tech defaults fail on deep skin.

## Timeline with dates
- **1940s–1950s** — Kodak creates the "Shirley Card," a printed reference featuring Shirley Page, a white Kodak employee. It becomes "the standard for a successfully calibrated photograph in terms of light and color." Lab printers set exposure/color balance to make *that* skin correct.
- **1960s–1980s** — Labs keep using Shirley Cards. Consequence stated plainly: "the darker the skin, the more invisible the subject looked." Mixed-race group photos rendered Black subjects "nearly reduced to a floating pair of teeth and eyes" — i.e., only the specular highlights on teeth and sclera survived the film's latitude.
- **1980s** — Kodak Gold Max introduced, marketed as able to photograph "a dark horse in low light" — the film-chemistry fix arrived for commercial, not equity, reasons.
- **1990s** — Kodak issues a **multiracial Shirley Card** (Asian, white, and Black women; a Hispanic woman added later).

## Why it changed
Kodak responded to complaints from **furniture and chocolate manufacturers** who couldn't advertise dark-brown products, not from families of color.

## Modern carryover
Digital removed the physical calibration card, but the bias persists in defaults: automated exposure metering, autofocus, and auto-white-balance "struggle to identify darker skin," because decades of "white is normal" tuning shaped the reference data and the algorithms trained on it.

## Craft implication
Correct rendering of deep skin is an act of **intent**, not default: as cited via *Moonlight* and *Insecure*, "Photographers and directors have to *want* their Black and darker-skinned subjects and actors to look good" — meaning deliberate exposure placement and lighting rather than trusting auto modes.
