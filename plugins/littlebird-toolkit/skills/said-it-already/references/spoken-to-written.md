# Spoken register to written register

The central craft problem of this skill. A line that landed in conversation frequently
reads badly on the page, and the reason is not that the speaker was sloppy. It is that
speech and writing are different systems that select different structures for the same
meaning.

**The operating rule: extraction captures the IDEA and the ENERGY. Drafting rebuilds it
for reading. Never paste a transcript line as a post.**

---

## What the measurement actually says

This is the best-evidenced section in the whole skill. Use it.

**The overlap between spoken and written phrasing is near zero.** Of the 133 four-word
lexical bundles occurring above 20 times per million words, only four overlapped between
conversation and academic prose, roughly 3%
(`research/distilled-content-mining-and-repurposing.md`, section 1). The two registers
barely share building blocks.

**The structures are opposed.** Speech clusters verbs, finite dependent clauses, pronouns,
and adverbials. Writing clusters nouns, attributive adjectives, and prepositional phrases
(same source). Passive verbs run roughly 2,000 per million words in conversation against
roughly 18,000 in academic writing. Conversation drops the that-complementizer; written
prose keeps it. "I" and "you" are among the most frequent words in spoken corpora and
considerably less frequent in written ones.

**Disfluency is the baseline, not a defect.** Ordinary conversation runs about 5.97
disfluencies per 100 words, roughly 6% of words
(`research/distilled-content-mining-and-repurposing.md`, section 1). A 500-word passage
carries around thirty of them before you touch structure.

**And the good material is the messiest.** The speaker doing the explaining produced 7.00
disfluencies per 100 words against 4.93 for the listener, and abstract material produced
6.37 against 5.55 for familiar material (same source). The teaching explanation and the
moment someone works out something hard out loud are the highest-value seeds and the worst
verbatim. **Expect the best seeds to look bad. Rebuild them rather than dropping them.**

## The five things a listener had that a reader will not

This is the practical checklist. Every one of them has to be repaired or removed.

1. **The preceding turn.** The user was answering something. The reader did not hear the
   question. Either fold the question into the opening or make the line stand alone.
2. **The shared frame.** "That thing we talked about last week", "the project", "their
   guy". A listener resolves these instantly. A reader cannot resolve them at all.
3. **The face.** Tone, the pause before the punchline, the raised eyebrow that marked the
   line as a joke. Written text has to carry that in the words or lose it. This is where
   sarcasm goes wrong most often.
4. **The ability to interrupt.** The listener could say "wait, what?" so the speaker could
   afford to be imprecise. A reader just stops reading.
5. **Patience.** A listener sits through the setup because leaving is awkward. A reader
   leaves in one line. Every source in the archive that talks about openings says the same
   thing from a different tradition: lead with the strongest line and never open with a
   context-setting intro (`research/distilled-content-mining-and-repurposing.md`,
   section 3).

## Cut, keep, rebuild

The line between mechanical cleanup and rewriting is established practice
(`research/distilled-content-mining-and-repurposing.md`, section 1). Cleanup preserves the
speaker. Rewriting replaces them. Know which one you are doing.

### Cut without hesitation

| Thing | Example |
|---|---|
| Fillers | um, uh, like, you know, I mean, sort of, right? |
| Stumble repeats | "I, I, I went" becomes "I went" |
| False starts that go nowhere | "So the way we, well, actually let me back up" |
| Verbal check-ins | "does that make sense?", "if that makes sense", "you know what I mean?" |
| Turn-taking scaffolding | "yeah so", "anyway", "to your point" |
| Redundant re-explanations | The second and third pass the speaker made because the listener looked lost |
| Deictic references the reader cannot resolve | "this thing here", "that", "them" with no antecedent |

### Keep, on purpose

| Thing | Why |
|---|---|
| **Word choice** | Never substitute synonyms. Swapping "scared" for "concerned" changes emotional weight (`research/distilled-content-mining-and-repurposing.md`, section 1). Their words are the point. |
| **Emphasis repetition** | "very, very cold" stays. Only stumble repetition goes (same source). |
| **Nonstandard grammar and dialect** | It carries identity. It is not an error to fix (same source). |
| **The concrete detail** | The specific number, the physical thing, the named month. Vivid imagery is a measured driver of belief change (same, section 4). Strip it and the piece stops working. |
| **The hedge** | "Roughly", "about", "I think". Removing a hedge converts an honest approximation into a false claim. |
| **A meaningful self-correction** | "It was six weeks, no, closer to eight" can be better than either number alone, because it shows a person being accurate rather than impressive (same, section 1). |
| **One rough edge** | Leave one. Perfectly smooth prose reads generated. This is the same rule the voice skills in this repo enforce. |

### Rebuild, always

Rebuilding is not cleanup. These are structural conversions.

**Pronoun density.** Speech leans on "I" and "you"
(`research/distilled-content-mining-and-repurposing.md`, section 1). Written prose that
keeps every one of them reads like a transcript. Cut the ones doing no work, keep the ones
carrying the relationship. Do not eliminate them: this is personal writing, not academic
prose, and stripping first person is the opposite error.

**Clause chains into sentences.** Speech chains clauses with "and", "so", "but", "because"
until the thought runs out. Break the chain. Vary the lengths violently. Add minimal
punctuation and break thoughts into units:

- Spoken: "and then we got there it was dark already my mom said don't go in"
- Written: "And then we got there. It was dark already, and my mom said, 'Don't go in.'"

That example is straight from transcript-editing practice
(`research/distilled-content-mining-and-repurposing.md`, section 1) and it is exactly the
operation required.

**Order.** Spoken stories arrive out of order because the speaker remembered a detail late
and backed up. Impose sequence. Randomly re-arranged story parts measurably reduce
transportation (`research/distilled-content-mining-and-repurposing.md`, section 4). For
client stories, the three-beat spine handles this: situation, complication, resolution,
with situation and complication kept short (same, section 4).

**The opening.** The spoken version almost never starts where the written version should.
Find the strongest line in the passage and move it to the front. Journalism craft and B2B
video marketing converge on this independently (same, section 3).

**Paragraphing.** Break at topic shifts (same, section 1). Speech has no paragraphs, and
an unbroken block loses a reader before the content matters.

## The three things that will ruin the rebuild

**1. Inflating a plain line into dramatic prose.** This is the one with measurement behind
it. Participants who perceived narrative material as "cheesy or oversentimental"
experienced measurably lower transportation
(`research/distilled-content-mining-and-repurposing.md`, section 4). Overwriting does not
just fail to help, it actively reduces the effect. If the user said "that was annoying",
they did not say "it was a moment of profound frustration".

**2. Telling instead of showing.** An editor quoted in the archive names words like
"tragic, staggering, or amazing" as exactly the wrong move
(`research/distilled-content-mining-and-repurposing.md`, section 3). If the moment was
surprising, let the fact be surprising.

**3. Losing the energy while fixing the grammar.** The reason the line was worth mining is
that it had force in the room. A grammatically perfect version with the force removed is a
worse post than the raw transcript, because at least the raw transcript was alive. If the
rebuild reads flat, go back to the verbatim, find the word carrying the heat, and build
the sentence around that word instead.

## Show your work

Every seed in the artifact shows the verbatim and the draft together, in that order,
labeled. Declaring the editing level explicitly is standard transcript practice
(`research/distilled-content-mining-and-repurposing.md`, section 1) and it does three
things here:

1. The user can see whether the rebuild kept them or replaced them.
2. It makes the composite failure impossible to hide, because one verbatim maps to one
   draft.
3. It teaches. After a few weeks the user starts recognizing their own good moments in
   real time, which is a better outcome than any individual post.

## Worked example

**Verbatim** (teaching explanation, tier 1, hypothetical shape only):

> "So the, the thing is, most people think the, uh, the hard part is picking the tool,
> right? And it's not, it's really not. Like we've done this now with, I want to say
> around thirty companies, and every single time the tool was fine. The tool was fine.
> What breaks it is nobody owns it. Does that make sense? Like there's no one person whose
> job it is when it goes sideways. So it just sits there."

**What happens to it.**

- Cut: "so the, the", "uh, the", "right?", "Does that make sense?", "Like" as filler.
- Keep: "The tool was fine. The tool was fine." That repeat is emphasis, not a stumble.
  Keep "I want to say around thirty", because the hedge is the honest form of the number.
  Keep "goes sideways" and "just sits there", which are the user's words and carry the heat.
- Rebuild: move the strongest line forward, break the clause chain, impose order.

**Draft** (short form):

> Most people think the hard part is picking the tool.
>
> We have done this with around thirty companies now. Every single time, the tool was
> fine. The tool was fine.
>
> What breaks it is that nobody owns it. There is no one person whose job it is when it
> goes sideways. So it just sits there.

Same idea, same energy, same words where the words mattered. Zero transcript texture. The
hedge survived. The repeat survived because it was doing work.
