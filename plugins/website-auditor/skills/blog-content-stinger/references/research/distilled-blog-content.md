Distilled research for the conditional, wave-W6a blog audit this Stinger runs when a blog is detected: word count and a `[subjective]` quality read on the 10 most recent posts, plus AI-authorship-probability analysis that this Stinger's own binding requirement says must ALWAYS be reported as a probability band with a stated method and error rate, never a flat verdict. Coverage is thin: only two raw sources exist, both filed under the single research cluster "ai-content-detection," one an academic preprint (arXiv, not yet peer-reviewed) and one a peer-reviewed, published journal article (Springer, International Journal for Educational Integrity). Both study AI-content detection in academic-integrity contexts (student essays, EFL writing), neither source studies detection of AI-authored marketing or blog content specifically, so every accuracy figure below should be read as evidence from an adjacent domain, not a direct measurement of blog-post detection, and reported with that caveat.

Research window: single sweep, 2026-08-18.

## 1. Why a probability band, not a verdict, is the only defensible framing

The arXiv paper's central finding is a direct, sourced justification for this Stinger's binding requirement: benchmarking multiple AI-generated-text detectors against its own AITDNA dataset, the authors "find that they often only perform well for specific notions but not as broad detectors." [raw/arxiv-org-html-2606-04906v1.md] The paper identifies five existing notions of what counts as "AI-generated text" (document-level binary detection, sentence-level detection, human/AI boundary identification, among others named in the literature review) and adds two new ones, content-ID-based and authorship-ID-based detection, arguing that "existing AITD notions in datasets and benchmarks are misaligned with real-world AI use and AI policies" and are "commonly under-specified, making findings on different datasets incompatible." [raw/arxiv-org-html-2606-04906v1.md] Practical consequence for this Stinger: an accuracy or error-rate figure from any detector or study is only meaningful alongside the specific notion, dataset, and definitional assumptions it was measured under, exactly the "method" component this Stinger's binding requirement demands be stated alongside every probability band.

## 2. Detector accuracy, with method and dataset stated (the only concrete numbers in this archive)

The Springer article evaluated two commercial detectors, Turnitin and Originality, against a balanced 192-text dataset composed of pre-GenAI authentic EFL (English as a Foreign Language) student writing, professional human-authored texts, AI-generated outputs, and hybrid (human-AI co-authored) compositions, with detector outputs categorized into Human, Hybrid, or AI using "established thresholding ranges" and performance assessed via standard classification metrics plus statistical significance tests. [raw/link-springer-com-article-10-1007-s40979-026-00213-1.md]

| Detector | Method / dataset | Overall accuracy | Macro-average recall | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Originality | 192-text balanced dataset (EFL student writing, professional human text, AI text, hybrid text), thresholding-range classification into Human/Hybrid/AI | 0.69 | 0.60 | [raw/link-springer-com-article-10-1007-s40979-026-00213-1.md] |
| Turnitin | Same 192-text dataset and method | 0.61 | 0.51 | [raw/link-springer-com-article-10-1007-s40979-026-00213-1.md] |

Both detectors "performed poorly on Hybrid texts, an increasingly common form of student writing, indicating substantial difficulty distinguishing mixed authorship." [raw/link-springer-com-article-10-1007-s40979-026-00213-1.md] Performance "declined significantly" with increased text length and with genre variation, both detectors scored "noticeably lower" on scientific writing than on humanities texts. [raw/link-springer-com-article-10-1007-s40979-026-00213-1.md] This is the single most citable accuracy-with-method data point in the archive, and any probability-band claim this Stinger's audit output makes about a specific detector should trace to this study and state the 0.69/0.61 accuracy range and the EFL-academic-writing context it was measured in, not present it as a universal detector accuracy figure.

## 3. Fairness risk: detectors may be biased against non-native English writers

The Springer study reports "a borderline trend toward higher accuracy" for Originality on professionally written texts compared to EFL student writing, "suggesting potential fairness concerns," and states plainly that both detectors' limitations mean they are "unsuitable as the sole basis for decisions regarding academic misconduct," recommending they "serve only as indicators that prompt further inquiry." [raw/link-springer-com-article-10-1007-s40979-026-00213-1.md] The paper's introduction separately cites, secondhand, other researchers (Deep et al. 2025) reporting "documented biases against non-native writers" in AI-detector accuracy generally. [raw/link-springer-com-article-10-1007-s40979-026-00213-1.md] Audit implication: an AI-authorship probability-band finding should not be phrased in a way that implies elevated confidence for content written by or edited for non-native English speakers, the evidence in this archive points the other way (potential bias, not superior detection reliability).

## 4. Human raters are not meaningfully more accurate than the detectors

The Springer article's introduction summarizes several cited studies (not independently verified by this Stinger's own raw archive, these are the Springer paper's own literature-review citations, reported here as that paper's framing, not as separately-fetched primary sources) on human ability to distinguish AI from human writing without detection tools:

| Study cited within the Springer article | Human detection accuracy | Note | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Waltzer et al. (2024) | Instructors correctly identified ChatGPT-generated work "only 70% of the time" | Instructor raters | [raw/link-springer-com-article-10-1007-s40979-026-00213-1.md] |
| Doru et al. (2025) | Overall 70% detection accuracy; medical experts 72%, humanities experts 65% | German-language medical-student essays vs. ChatGPT-3.5 output, semi-randomized controlled design, no detection tools used | [raw/link-springer-com-article-10-1007-s40979-026-00213-1.md] |
| Elkhatat et al. (2023) | Not quantified in the archived text beyond "humans struggle to reliably differentiate" | General finding | [raw/link-springer-com-article-10-1007-s40979-026-00213-1.md] |

These figures (roughly 65 to 72% human accuracy, cited secondhand within the Springer article) sit in a similar range to the Originality/Turnitin machine-detector accuracy figures in section 2 (0.61 to 0.69), which is useful context for calibrating how confident any single-source probability-band claim should sound: neither trained human raters nor commercial detectors in these studies exceed roughly 70 to 72% accuracy on the specific tasks measured. [raw/link-springer-com-article-10-1007-s40979-026-00213-1.md]

## 5. Hybrid, human-AI co-authored text is the hardest and most realistic case

Both raw sources converge on this point from different angles, the strongest agreement in this thin archive. The Springer study found both commercial detectors "performed poorly on Hybrid texts," and describes hybrid composition as "an increasingly common form of student writing." [raw/link-springer-com-article-10-1007-s40979-026-00213-1.md] The arXiv paper's entire dataset contribution, AITDNA (350-plus texts, 99 human authors, recording the full prompt and edit history of human-AI interaction), exists specifically because "existing AITD datasets do not represent natural human-AI co-creation and encode unrealistic assumptions," and the paper states that human-AI co-writing "has emerged as common writing pattern across domains," with authorship "specific to each token" in a jointly-written text rather than cleanly attributable to one party. [raw/arxiv-org-html-2606-04906v1.md] For blog content specifically, where AI-assisted drafting, editing, and outlining are common and largely undocumented in either source, this is the most directly transferable finding in the archive: any AI-authorship probability-band finding should account for the strong likelihood of partial, mixed authorship rather than presenting a binary "written by AI" or "written by a human" frame, since that binary is exactly what both sources found detectors and human raters alike are worst at resolving.

## 6. Gaps

Neither source measures detector or human accuracy on marketing or blog-style content, both study academic essay contexts (EFL student writing, medical-student essays, humanities and scientific academic genres). Neither source names a specific error-rate or false-positive-rate figure broken out separately from overall accuracy, "0.69 accuracy" and "0.61 accuracy" are the closest figures to a stated error rate available in this archive, and should be reported as such (implying roughly 31% and 39% misclassification rates respectively on the Springer study's own dataset) rather than as a general-purpose false-positive rate. Neither source documents any specific commercial AI-detection tool beyond Turnitin and Originality, so no probability-band claim in this Stinger's output should be attributed to any other named detector (GPTZero, Copyleaks, etc.) without a dedicated source.
